Skills Guide

37 skills for writing a novel, built on a craft base extracted from 39 writing lectures. Type the command in chat. Each skill loads only the craft documents it needs, so you never carry the whole base while drafting.

Read the tier before you trust the output

Every skill declares its tier when you run it. This is the most important thing on this page — a system equally confident about everything will mislead you.

TierWhat it doesHow much to trust it
1 · Mechanical Verifies against a documented rule High. There's a right answer. It reports findings flatly and never fixes anything
2 · Diagnostic Maps a symptom to likely causes using documented base rates Good. Gives ranked hypotheses, not verdicts. You confirm which is real
3 · Procedural Runs a documented multi-stage process in order Good for the process. The stages are the value; it won't let you skip ahead
4 · Generative Produces raw material Lowest. It says so. Output is material to react against, never an answer
If you're starting from nothing
  1. /brainstorm → /new-story → /premise-test — is this a novel or just a setting?
  2. /magic-system, /worldbuild, /new-character — build what the story needs, not everything
  3. /outline or /structure — as much plan as you actually want
  4. /write — the drafting loop. This is where most time goes
  5. /threads, /promises, /plants — run these on the outline, before drafting. They work without prose
  6. Something's wrong → /diagnose. It's the front door and routes to the rest
  7. Draft done → /revise — and it will stop you polishing too early

Starting

Idea to premise, before there's a project

/new-story Tier 4 · Generative
Take an idea from spark to premise, then test it against Sanderson's three-pillar readiness gate before it becomes a project.
whenUse when starting a brand-new story from an idea, when deciding whether a concept is ready to be a novel, when story/premise.md doesn't exist yet, or when the author asks "is this enough to start on?"
what it promises
Tier 4 — generative. The readiness gate and its threshold are Sanderson's. Everything I draft toward a premise — wording, pillar ideas, the "this meets this" line — is raw material for your judgement, not an answer. Where a pillar is thin, that's the finding. I won't invent an idea to fill it.

/brainstorm Tier 4 · Generative
Generate atomic ideas and combine them into strange attractors — the raw fragments a premise gets built from.
whenUse when starting from nothing, when an idea feels thin or generic, when looking for a "this meets this" combination, or when the author wants options instead of one finished concept. Not for developing a premise already in hand — see /new-story for that.
what it promises
Tier 4 — generative. Every idea below is a fragment to react to, reject, or recombine — not a premise, and not a recommendation. I keep them deliberately unfinished. Finishing one into a premise is `/new-story`'s job, done on purpose, later.

Tier 2 — diagnostic. The six questions, and the transplant read on question five, are the test. My score on how well the premise clears each one is a ranked hypothesis, not a verdict — the author is the one who knows whether a dilemma is genuinely unique to this premise.

Audit every open narrative thread and verify each one closes, in the right order.
whenUse when an ending feels unsatisfying, when a draft or outline feels like it's missing something you can't name, or before calling a chapter, act, or book finished. Also use when the author asks about loose ends, dangling plots, or unresolved subplots.

Find "gorillas in the phone booth" — unsignalled oddities that pull a reader out of the story to ask a question the narrative hasn't promised to answer. Use after drafting a scene, when a beta reader reports getting confused or distracted at a specific spot, before finishing a chapter, or when the author asks whether something needs a lantern hung on it. /beta-read Tier 2/4 · Diagnostic / Generative
Read a draft as a reader and report back — either as an alpha reader who diagnoses problems, or as a beta reader who reports pure reaction.

/polish Tier 3 · Procedural
Run the 5.0 polish pass — cut roughly 10% of length, eliminate passive voice, upgrade weak verbs to strong ones, then copyedit and proofread. Refuses to run if structure isn't locked and explains why, unless the author overrules. Use only after the revision map's top and middle tiers are resolved and any beta-driven changes are in, when a chapter is ready for a final line pass, or when the author explicitly asks to polish prose.
what it promises
Tier 3 — procedural, and this one has a gate. It refuses to run on structurally unresolved material and explains the cost before the author overrules it. Once running, it follows the documented order — cut, then passive voice, then weak verbs, then copyedit — and doesn't reorder it.

/query Tier 4 · Generative
Draft a query letter — hook, comp titles, brief credentials, and housekeeping (title, word count, genre).
whenUse when preparing to query literary agents, when the manuscript is finished and ready to submit, or when practicing the hook paragraph. Flags that specific market conventions drift and directs the author to the target agent's current guidelines rather than asserting norms.

/synopsis Tier 4 · Generative
Write a full synopsis — the entire plot including the ending, in present tense, with nothing withheld — showing the causal chain rather than a list of events.
whenUse when an agent or editor requests a synopsis alongside a query, when the author wants to test whether the plot's cause-and-effect actually holds together, or after the manuscript's structure is locked.
what it promises
Tier 4 — generative. Raw material to react against, not a finished document. But this genre has one hard formal rule the other Tier 4 skills don't: nothing is withheld. A synopsis that holds back the ending isn't a weaker synopsis — it's not doing the job.
