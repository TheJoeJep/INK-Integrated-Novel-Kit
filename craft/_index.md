## The three questions

Before consulting anything else, on any problem:

1. **What did this promise, and did it pay?**
2. **What does this character want, and does it show?**
3. **What progressed here, and can the reader see it?**

## How to read this

**As a toolkit, not a rulebook.** His own strongest caution is that treating any framework as
sacrosanct makes you a cook rather than a chef — you'll write stories that don't fit your vision.

The question to ask of anything here is not *is this true?* It is:

> **What problem is this designed to solve, and do I currently have that problem?**

### Don't try to hold all of this at once

**His own instruction, and it governs how this base should be used.** He borrows it from the
Magic: The Gathering player Luis Scott-Vargas: when you learn a card game, the basic rules take
all your attention. As they become second nature, brain space frees up for higher-level play.

> As a new writer, the hardest thing is doing it consistently — just sitting down and writing.
> That's often all you can do. If you try to keep all of this in mind, you'll be overwhelmed and
> won't get anything done.

Build the habit. Then add **one** thing to focus on. When it's instinct, add another.

Most of this base is a **diagnostic kit for when something breaks**, or an outlining aid — not a
checklist to run while drafting.

### The diagnostic habit — **[not from the lectures]**

> **Sourcing correction.** This checklist is **not Sanderson's.** It came from an earlier working
> summary of Lecture 1 and, when the transcript was checked directly, no such checklist appears
> anywhere in it. Two other tidy frameworks from that same summary — an ordered revision queue and
> a three-tier model of reader feedback — also turned out not to be his.
>
> It's kept because it's genuinely useful, and it's consistent with his actual position that
> advice is individual rather than universal. But it is **this project's invention**, not his
> teaching, and it should not be cited as his. See [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

Apply this to every rule in this base:

1. What reader expectation does this create?
2. What emotional response is it trying to produce?
3. What usually goes wrong without it?
4. Under what circumstances would breaking it improve the story?
5. What would I need to provide instead?
