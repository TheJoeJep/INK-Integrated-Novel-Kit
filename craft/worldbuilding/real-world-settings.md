# Real-World and Contemporary Settings

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 9 — *How To Worldbuild on Earth?* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**No official recap exists for this lecture.** The [extraction notes](../../docs/sources/notes/2025-11-how-to-worldbuild-on-earth.md)
are the only record of it.
**Reach for this when:** writing urban fantasy, secret history, contemporary or historical
settings — anything where the reader can check you.

> This is the **only** lecture in the course that addresses real-world settings. Everything else
> in this craft base assumes an invented world.

---

## The core asymmetry

**Getting real-world detail wrong is far easier than getting invented-world detail wrong, and the
odds an expert catches you go up enormously.**

- **Jim Butcher** gets complaints from Chicago residents about the city's layout in the *Dresden
  Files* — he wasn't living there when he wrote it
- **World War II novels are famously hard to write**, because the population of armchair experts
  is vast. Small details carry the weight of the book's whole claim to respectability

**Why one error costs so much** — from a military veteran in the room: once a reader catches one
fundamental mistake, **they stop trusting your judgment on everything else**, including the
character work. It isn't proportional damage. It's a trust collapse.

---

## Bonehead characters — the best technique here

Two responses to that risk. Raise your research, or:

**Give your viewpoint character a plausible, characterized reason not to know the domain.**

**His example: Kaladin doesn't know horses.** Neither does Sanderson — his wife does. Rather than
fake expertise, he built a character trait around the gap.

**Why it works:** readers forgive a gap that is *characterized* rather than *accidental*. And it
stays correctable — the character can be taught later, in-story.

This converts a research weakness into characterization. For a contemporary novel where your
protagonist is an ordinary person encountering institutions, science, or procedure they've no
business understanding, it's close to free.

---

## How much research is enough

**The model, as a rule of thumb:**

| Effort | Gets you |
|---|---|
| First weeks/months | **~50% of the way to expertise.** Early gains are cheap |
| **~10× that again** | 50% → 75% |
| Beyond | Micro-increments |

**His actual process:**

1. Research until you're confident your likely mistakes are **fixable without undermining the
   narrative**
2. **Write the story**
3. Find a genuine expert to flag what's wrong, and revise on demand

Rather than spending years becoming an expert before drafting. He notes two cases where this bit
him — he underestimated his own competence and needed more correction than expected.

**Good alpha and beta readers exist for exactly this category of error.** See
[../process/feedback-and-critique.md](../process/feedback-and-critique.md).

## Writing real cultures

**His caution, worded strongly:** fantasy writers habitually borrow real, living belief systems —
his named example is Navajo belief folded into invented magic systems — and treat them as free
material in a way that would read as obviously condescending if done to most other religions.

**The problem isn't the borrowing. It's the selectivity** — that pop culture has decided some
cultures are available to raid and others aren't.

**His cardinal rule:**

> I want people to depict me well and do the research for me — so I want to make sure I'm
> depicting them well.

### Conscious vs. accidental

**The operative distinction is not right vs. wrong.** You can make a choice others would object
to. The ground you stand on is far stronger if you made it **consciously** and can defend it.

**His example against himself, and it's a good one:**

- **Bridge Four has no women.** A *conscious, defensible* choice — wartime forced-labour
  conscription. Another author could argue it differently. It was a decision
- **The Mistborn crew was entirely male.** He built Vin as a dynamic character, partly from his
  14-year-old sister — and only noticed **after finishing** that everyone else was a man. He'd
  absorbed it from every heist film he'd seen. **Unconscious**, and already in print

Same surface fact, completely different footing.

## Contrast is your friend

**For building:** when constructing two religions or cultures, make them **contrasting facets of
the same underlying thing**, or give them opposed views on something central to the plot. Readers
grasp both faster.

**For pitching:** real-world-set fantasy compresses to one line via contrast.

**His example:** the film *Yesterday* — the Beatles never existed, one man remembers them, nobody
else does. *Boom, you have a pitch.*

**The prescription:** ask **what's different between your world and ours, what's the same**, and
build outward from there.

---

## The learning curve is a choice

Genre shortcuts still apply in real-world and SF settings. A science fiction audience will accept
*this society uses stable wormholes, don't ask* without complaint. You don't have to explain
American football either.

**Every book has a learning curve regardless of setting, and you choose where to spend it.**
Steeper signals a harder, denser book; shallower gives an easy on-ramp.

## Known gaps — now confirmed across the whole corpus

**The masquerade** — why the wider world hasn't noticed the fantastic element — and **alternate /
secret history technique** are **not covered anywhere in any of the 39 lectures.**

That's not an assumption. Three separate sources where it would naturally appear were checked
directly and all came up empty: this lecture, the 2025 Worldbuilding Q&A, and the 2020
Worldbuilding Q&A.

**The closest adjacent material**, and it isn't the same thing: Saberhagen's *Books of Swords*
comes up in the 2020 Q&A — advanced humans mistaken for gods, revealed gradually. But that
constructs a **secret history inside an invented world**, not the real-world *why hasn't anyone
noticed* problem.

**What this means practically.** If you're writing urban fantasy, secret history, or any premise
where the fantastic is hidden in the real world, **the craft base has nothing for you on the
central structural problem of that genre.** Don't infer his position. It isn't recorded.

## Checks

- [ ] What real-world domains does this story touch that a reader could check you on?
- [ ] For each — research, or a **bonehead character** with a characterized reason not to know?
- [ ] Are you at ~50% and drafting, or over-researching before you start?
- [ ] Do you have a genuine expert reader lined up for the revision pass?
- [ ] Any real culture or belief system being borrowed? Is the choice **conscious and
      defensible**, or absorbed from other fiction without noticing?
- [ ] What's different between your world and ours, and what's the same?
- [ ] Can you state the premise in one contrast line?
- [ ] How steep is your learning curve, and did you choose it?

## Failure modes

- **The single fatal detail.** One error collapses trust in everything, including the characters
- **Faked expertise.** Worse than an admitted gap, because it's uncharacterized
- **Research as avoidance.** Years to 75% instead of months to 50% and a draft
- **Unconscious absorption.** The all-male heist crew you didn't notice writing
- **Assuming this doc covers the masquerade.** It doesn't
