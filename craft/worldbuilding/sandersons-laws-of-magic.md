# Sanderson's Laws of Magic

**Source:** Sanderson, 2020 Lecture 5 (the full Laws lecture) and 2025 Lecture 7 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcripts)
**Reach for this when:** designing any speculative system — magic, technology, or any invented
capability — or when a system is eating the plot.

> **His own framing.** He named them after himself because "Moore has laws, Clarke has at least
> one." They are observations about what made *his* systems better. He breaks them. His closing
> line in 2020: **if these rules help you tell a great story, great — if they don't, throw them
> away.**

---

## Zeroth Law — Always err on the side of what is awesome

**Taught in 2020. Absent from the 2025 lecture and from the official recap — but it is real, and
it comes first.**

Stories rarely start for him from worldbuilding machinery. They start from **a cool image he
works backward from.**

*The Way of Kings* began partly from *power armour is cool, I want fantasy power armour — how do
I make that make sense?* and *giant magic swords are cool but make no narrative sense in most
settings — how do I build a setting where you'd actually want one?*

**The order matters.** Awesome first, justification second. The other three Laws are about making
the awesome thing hold up — not about generating it.

## First Law — Understanding equals magic

**The wording is near-identical across both courses:**

> Your ability to solve problems with magic in a satisfying way is directly proportional to how
> well the reader understands said magic.

**What kind of law it is — he answers differently across years, and both are useful:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **2020** | *"Sanderson's First Law is really a law of foreshadowing."* Stated twice |
| **2025** | A **plotting law** |

Not a rename — two characterizations of the same rule. The foreshadowing framing is the more
actionable one: **the goal is that the reader *could have* worked out the resolution beforehand,
even if they didn't.**

### Where it came from

After *Mistborn* book one, his editor said the ending needed "a little extra oomph." His fix was
to move up a foreshadowed book-three beat — Vin learning to push on the mists — so it lands at the
climax.

Which, he realised after publication, means **giving the protagonist a new power at the 95% mark.**
He says plainly: that's exactly what I did.

*(And he defends SFF against the "anything can happen" charge: any genre patches endings this way.
His example — a romance where a long-lost uncle reveals the commoner is secretly nobility at 90%,
erasing the class conflict.)*

### The proof it's about promises, not mechanics

**His *Lord of the Rings* pair**, and it's the sharpest thing in the lecture:

- ***The Two Towers*** — Gandalf promises *look for me at the morning of the fifth day*, then five
  days of desperate fighting make you forget, and he delivers exactly what was set up
- ***The Return of the King*** — Aragorn recruits the army of the dead with **no equivalent
  promise to the audience.** Structurally the same rescue. It lands as *oh, okay, I guess they're
  fine*

**His diagnosis:** this is promise and payoff, not magic comprehension. **You need to understand
what was promised, not the mechanics.** See [../plot/payoff-and-twists.md](../plot/payoff-and-twists.md).

### When it deliberately doesn't apply

A character introduced in act one by dramatically saving everyone — Han Solo — works, because it
**establishes character** rather than resolving tension. The same rescue in act three, unearned,
fails.

### The honest caveat

> You can totally read one of my books and say, Sanderson, I don't think you completely pulled
> that off.

## Hard and soft magic

**The spectrum, not a binary** — most stories sit between, and he flags it himself as not-quite-a-law.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Hard** | The reader can learn the rules; they're internally consistent; the story is about mastering and applying them. Magic as another branch of science |
| **Soft** | The reader can't know all the rules; wonder or horror accompanies its use; outcomes can't be anticipated |

### Where the distinction came from

His first professional convention panel, Boston, around 2004–05. Asked for his go-to basic on
magic design, he said **"you need rules"** — expecting agreement. The rest of the panel, all urban
fantasy writers, told him rules ruin magic.

Thirty minutes of arguing before he understood: **they were writing soft magic for good reason.**
Urban fantasy has one foot in horror, and horror requires the reader to lack control.

### Tolkien runs both ends at once

**The One Ring is nearly hard magic** — invisibility, lifespan drain, Sauron senses you, eventual
corruption. And Tolkien **puts Gollum on the page so the reader can see the endpoint.**

**Gandalf is almost entirely soft** — undefined power, his biggest moments off-screen, and his
purpose isn't tactical. It's to make the hobbits feel small in a world larger than they can
comprehend.

*(Second example: Rothfuss — sympathy taught at the University is hard, engineering-like; Naming
is soft, "likely to drive you crazy," the poetry discipline the protagonist is drawn to like a
moth to flame.)*

**Rule of thumb for series:** stay mostly hard, but leave **one mystery tool unexplained** in book
one, so a later book has room to expand it.

## Second Law — Limitations are more interesting than powers

> Flaws or limitations are more interesting than powers.

**He states this as a character principle first**, then applies it to magic. Superman; and why the
story follows Frodo rather than Gandalf. The magic and character versions are **one idea**, not
two parallel frameworks.

**The proof:** full telekinesis is strictly better than allomancy — and makes for *less*
interesting books than allomancy's constrained push/pull-through-centre-of-mass. He's watched it
happen.

**The minimal demonstration:** *you can fly* is a fine story starter. *You can fly, but only while
your parents are asleep* immediately generates plot — do I need to move to the other side of the
world to widen my window?

### The three-way split — and its four names

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Flaw** | Fixable through growth or effort. Luke losing his hand — he doesn't need a better sword-arm, he needs to stop relying on aggression, and the third film works because the flaw is actually overcome |
| **Limitation** | Structural, not meant to be fixed, worked within. Allomancy's centre-of-mass rule. *The Rithmatist* — some people have it, some don't, and "life isn't fair" is the theme; the climax works *with* the limitation |
| **Cost** | A price paid per use. Stormlight; a gunslinger's bullets; **a close relative dies** (attributed to Orson Scott Card); wishes that go wrong |

> **⚠️ He renames this taxonomy in every telling.** 2020 Laws: flaw / limitation / cost. 2025 L7:
> flaw / limitation / hindrance / cost. 2025 L3: flaw / handicap / challenge. 2025 L5:
> flaw / restriction / limitation. 2016: flaw / handicap only — no third category.
>
> **The definitions do not map one-to-one.** 2025's *handicap* (self-imposed, don't want it
> overcome) is not 2020's *limitation* (structural, not your fault). **Learn the concepts, not the
> labels** — see [../character/proactive-relatable-capable.md](../character/proactive-relatable-capable.md).

### Show the endpoint early

*The Wheel of Time* — channelling risks insanity and killing everyone you love, and **the prologue
opens by showing that outcome already happened to someone.** Same move as putting Gollum on the
page.

### The quirk-without-cost trap

A small "just for fun" power is fine as characterization — Luke never loses at holochess — **as
long as the story never puts weight on it.** The moment the plot needs him to win a tournament,
the earlier quirk becomes a liability.

**Related: the Eagles problem.** Enough readers ask why they didn't just fly the Eagles to Mordor
that the in-world answer clearly wasn't presented satisfyingly — even though it never bothered him
personally. See [../scene/scene-craft.md](../scene/scene-craft.md) on gorillas.

### Superman's three story shapes

Offered as why Superman is hard to adapt: (1) someone shows up who punches harder, (2) kryptonite
removes the powers, (3) **the powers simply can't solve this problem** — someone weaker is in
danger, or he wants to be loved for himself.

His example of writers who understood it: a *Lois & Clark* episode with a kryptonite-powered robot
where the actual story is a relationship problem, and the fight resolves in about ten seconds —
because the punching was never the interesting part.

## Third Law — Expand what you have before adding something new

> Before adding something new to your magic — and your setting in general — see if you can instead
> expand what you have.

### Where it came from: the failed *Way of Kings*

The **2002 draft**, written before he sold *Elantris*. Roughly **300,000 words**, attempting **ten
character arcs** and getting each about **10% of the way through** — and he'd been planning
**thirty magic systems** (ten on the Surges, ten on Voidbinding, plus fabrial mechanisms).

Chasing *bigger is better*. **The book failed.**

He rebuilt it from scratch after 2007, cutting to **three focal arcs** — Kaladin, Dalinar, Shallan
— with Kaladin taking over half the finished book. That worked.

### Three ways to expand

**Extrapolate.** Ask *what happens when*. How does this change society, economics, culture?

**Interconnect.** Tie the system to theme; connect powers to each other.

**Streamline.** Consolidate. Combine. Cut complexity.

### Two worked examples

- ***The Elder Scrolls.*** *Daggerfall*'s ten thousand procedurally-generated dungeons were built
  from a handful of recombined assets — reviewers called it *an ocean an inch deep*. *Skyrim*
  deliberately walked this back to fewer, hand-crafted dungeons and was better received
- **Three religions from one branch.** Rather than fifty shallow religions, invent three that fork
  from a shared root — he compares it to Christianity, Judaism and Islam — and dig into how their
  differing readings of that root create tension

## Checks

- [ ] What's the **awesome thing** this system exists to deliver? (Zeroth)
- [ ] Where on the hard/soft spectrum, and is that deliberate?
- [ ] Are you **solving** problems only with magic the reader understands?
- [ ] Are you **creating** problems with the magic they don't?
- [ ] Could a reader have worked out the resolution beforehand?
- [ ] Is the payoff resting on **understood mechanics** or on **a promise made**? (The Aragorn test)
- [ ] Flaws, limitations, and costs — all three named separately?
- [ ] Have you shown the reader the endpoint early, Gollum-style?
- [ ] Any quirk-without-cost the plot might later lean on?
- [ ] Extrapolated the societal consequences?
- [ ] **Adding a new power where you could expand an existing one?**
- [ ] How many arcs and how many systems? (Ten and thirty killed the first *Way of Kings*)

## Failure modes

- **Soft magic solving the climax.** Reads as authorial rescue
- **The 95% power.** A new capability arriving exactly when needed — he did this himself
- **Powers without limits.** Nothing to write scenes against
- **Costless magic.** No decision is ever interesting
- **Breadth over depth.** Thirty systems, none explored
- **The unweighted quirk that later matters**
- **Unextrapolated premises.** A world-changing capability that changed nothing
