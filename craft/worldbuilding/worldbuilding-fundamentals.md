# Worldbuilding Fundamentals

**Source:** Sanderson, [The Tools to Make Your Worlds Better, Lecture 8 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025) · **Tier:** PRIMARY
Plus 2020 course, Lecture 6 (SECONDARY). Real-world/contemporary settings are a known
[GAP](../SOURCES.md).
**Reach for this when:** building a world, or when worldbuilding is consuming time that should
go into the book.

---

## The governing principle

> Worldbuilding should serve the story, not hinder progress.

Worldbuilding is not about intricate histories and geographies. It's about a backdrop that
**enhances character journeys and plot progression.**

**The observation that reframes everything:**

> Some of the most immersive worlds in literature are not the most detailed, but the most
> cohesive.

Cohesion beats density. A world where three things interlock convincingly feels deeper than one
with two hundred unconnected facts.

And from the 2020 material (SECONDARY), stated bluntly: **excellent worldbuilding with bad
characters and story is worse than weak worldbuilding with strong characters and plot.**

---

## The three pillars converge

A novel comes together where strong ideas converge across **setting, character, and plot.** His
own threshold: he needs a couple of good ideas in each area before a novel feels like it's
coming together.

**Useful as a readiness check.** One brilliant idea in one pillar is not a novel.

---

## The three components

### 1. Physical setting
Exists without sentient beings.

- **Geography** — mountains and deserts that isolate regions
- **Climate and weather** — affecting food, clothing, architecture, daily life
- **Flora and fauna**, and how people interact with them
- **Cosmology** — celestial bodies; multiple moons affecting tides and mythology

### 2. Cultural setting
Shaped by inhabitants.

- Laws and government
- **Religion and belief** — influencing daily life and political power
- Technology level
- Language and dialect, reflecting history
- Social norms, traditions, taboos, holidays — **varying by class**

### 3. Magic system
See [sandersons-laws-of-magic.md](sandersons-laws-of-magic.md).

- Hard or soft
- Its social position — feared, revered, mundane
- Limitations and costs

> **⚠️ This third category is a 2025 addition.** In 2020 he taught a **two-way split — physical and
> cultural** — and when a student proposed magic as its own category, he **explicitly folded it
> into *physical* setting.**
>
> That isn't a relabel, and the older version has a real argument behind it: magic *is* part of how
> the physical world works. Treating it as a third pillar quietly implies it's bolted onto the
> world rather than native to it.
>
> **Use whichever fits your premise.** If your speculative element is framed as a discovered
> property of reality rather than a supernatural overlay, the 2020 two-way split describes your
> world more honestly.

## Worldbuilder's disease

**His term.** Spending so long planning that you become paralyzed by the scope of what's left to
do.

**The antidote:**
- Pick **key elements** for deep development instead of fleshing out everything
- Keep deadlines
- Work iteratively

The Third Law applies here directly: **expand what you have before adding something new.**

> It's not about density of ideas. It's about how the ideas influence each other and the depth
> you can play with them.

## The hollow iceberg

**He explicitly says the commonly-taught version is wrong**, and this correction only survives in
the 2020 lecture.

Other authors describe worldbuilding as an iceberg — a huge unseen mass beneath a small visible
tip. His correction:

> This is usually wrong. What we're actually doing is a **hollow iceberg.**

You do just enough work that, looking down through the water, **it appears to keep going** —
without ever building the submerged mass.

**The stage-magician framing:**

> Writing books is like being a stage magician. You're slowly giving them the information they
> need so you can punch them in the face later.

The reader should come away thinking *they've clearly done all that, I can trust them and just
enjoy this world* — **even though most of it was never built.**

**The exception he names: Tolkien**, who had twenty years and a linguistics career to build the
real thing. "There's nothing wrong with that" — but a working novelist publishing every few years
cannot afford it, and shouldn't pretend to.

**Why cohesion beats volume, restated:** a reader infers depth from *consistency*, not quantity.
Three details that clearly imply a fourth do more than twenty that imply nothing. The hollow
iceberg is that principle taken to its practical conclusion.

## Getting it onto the page

See [prose-and-exposition.md](../prose/prose-and-exposition.md) for the full treatment. The core
moves:

- **Pyramid of abstraction** — concrete sensory grounding earns abstract explanation
- **Describe how it feels**, not how it works. The shimmer of spice; the metallic taste of
  burning metals
- **Avoid the encyclopedia opening**
- **Filter through a character** — what someone notices characterizes them

## Case study — Mistborn

Worth following because it shows the pillars generating each other:

1. **Starting concept:** *what if the Dark Lord won?* — a plot idea that became a setting feature
2. **Logical exploration:** how would society function after total conquest? How would rebellion
   look? What norms would develop?
3. **Magic origin:** Allomancy was repurposed from an earlier unpublished novel, then refined to
   serve the needs of a **thieving crew** — the plot archetype shaped the magic
4. **Integration:** magic, society, and characters evolved together

**The takeaway:** the world feels alive because it was developed *in response to* the story's
needs rather than in advance of them. When world design harmonizes with character roles and
narrative stakes, believability follows.

## Where to start

Starting points vary legitimately — a single powerful concept, a character, or a plot need.

> Your approach is less important than ensuring your world supports rather than overshadows your
> core story.

**His own method:** start a document, make headings for the three pillars — usually setting,
character, plot — and build under them.

## Checks

- [ ] Does each worldbuilding element touch a character or the plot? If not, why is it in the
      document?
- [ ] Do you have a couple of solid ideas in **each** of the three pillars?
- [ ] Is this cohesive, or merely detailed?
- [ ] Are you expanding what exists, or adding something new?
- [ ] Have you extrapolated consequences — how does this change society, economy, daily life?
- [ ] Do social norms vary by class, or does everyone in this culture behave identically?
- [ ] Is the reader given sensation before system?
- [ ] Are you avoiding the work by worldbuilding? (Honest answer)

## Failure modes

- **Worldbuilder's disease.** Infinite preparation, no manuscript.
- **Density mistaken for depth.** Many facts, no interlock.
- **Orphan worldbuilding.** Elements that touch nothing in the story.
- **Monocultures.** One religion, one custom set, no class variation.
- **Unextrapolated premises.** A world-altering feature that altered nothing.
- **World over story.** Beautiful setting, thin characters. The trade that never pays.
