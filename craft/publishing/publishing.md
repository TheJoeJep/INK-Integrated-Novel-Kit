# Publishing

**Source:** Sanderson, [How Publishing Works, Lecture 10 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/publishing-industry-lecture-2025) and [What Publishing Does For You, Lecture 11 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/what-publishing-does-for-you-brandon-sandersons-writing-lecture-11-2025) · **Tier:** PRIMARY
**Reach for this when:** finishing a manuscript, querying, or choosing a publishing path.

---

## Traditional publishing

**Timeline:** 18–24 months from finished manuscript to shelf. Plan life around this.

### Who's involved

| Role | Does |
|---|---|
| Publisher | Final call on budget and direction |
| Editorial Director | Leads the imprint, decides acquisitions |
| **Editor** | Creative partner **and internal advocate** |
| Copyeditor / Proofreader | Grammar, style, formatting |
| Art and Design | Cover, typesetting, visual identity |
| Sales | Bookstore and library placement |
| Marketing / Publicity | Campaigns, ads, media |

### The warning that matters most

> Your editor is your lifeline. If you don't have someone in-house fighting for your book, it
> will get lost.

**Editors leaving mid-process leaves a book without a champion.** This is a real and common
career hazard, and it is largely why an agent matters.

### Money

- **Advance** — paid upfront against future royalties
- **Royalties** — typically **10–15% on hardcovers**
- **Earning out** — when royalties exceed the advance, further payments begin. A book that
  doesn't earn out still keeps its advance, but publishers may resist future deals
- Foreign rights, audio rights, and print run size all move the total

### The clarity worth internalizing

> The publisher's job is not to make you money. Their job is to make them money. Those things
> might align. They might not.

Not cynicism — orientation. It explains most publisher behavior that otherwise seems baffling.

## Agents

**Commission: 15%** (risen from 10%, reflecting expanded scope).

### Five functions

1. **Salesperson** — matching the manuscript to the right publisher and editor
2. **Business manager** — long-term career strategy
3. **Developmental editor** — refining before submission
4. **Negotiator** — contract terms
5. **Advocate** — represents the author's interests *exclusively*, and intervenes in conflicts
   with the publisher

**Take time choosing.** Fit matters over a career, not just for one deal.

**His example:** his agent Joshua pushed for greater *Elantris* bookstore stocking and insisted
on redesigning the *Mistborn* paperback cover — both credited with protecting his career.

---

## Rights to keep

- **Film rights — keep them.** Publishers generally have no expertise here
- **World and character rights** — control over future adaptations and expansions
- **Foreign rights** — retain where possible for global opportunities
- **Non-compete clauses** — he calls these *morally reprehensible* for how they restrict a
  writer's freedom to publish

## Pitching

### At conferences

Don't ask *what are you looking for?* Everyone asks that.

**Ask instead:** *what have you bought recently by a new author, and why?* Specific, answerable,
and it tells you what they actually acquire rather than what they say they want.

### The pitch itself

**Tailor to the listener.** His two versions of the same book:

**For a genre-familiar audience** — lead with the premise inversion: what if Frodo reached the
end, Sauron killed him, and took over the world? That's this world. Years later, a gang of
thieves plots to rob the Dark Lord.

**For a genre-unfamiliar audience** — lead with character: this is about a young girl named Vin
who discovers her lifelong streak of luck is actually a connection to a magic that comes from
metal.

**Components:** succinct but intriguing; carefully chosen comp titles; brief personal credibility
that doesn't overshadow the manuscript.

## Indie / self-publishing

**What changed:** Kindle Direct Publishing removed the printing press from the equation — you
need a Word document.

**Advantages:** far higher royalty share, dramatically lower production cost, and **speed** —
which is decisive in genres where readers consume 10+ books a week and won't wait a year for a
sequel.

**What it requires:** writing speed, metadata understanding, marketing platform mastery
(especially Amazon), and brand building.

**What it buys, in his words:** *control.*

### Audiobooks

A quiet revolution. **ACX** enables self-production or narrator royalty-sharing. Some indie
authors reportedly make six figures from audio alone. **Fantasy benefits disproportionately** —
long books are more accessible in audio.

### Crowdfunding

Only works when the **audience already exists.** Infrastructure should follow demand, never
precede it.

## Choosing a path

There is no universally correct answer. The path depends on the actual goal — income, audience,
or film/TV — and each suggests something different.

> There's no one right way to publish. But knowing how each model works — really works — gives
> you the power to choose what's best for you.

**His own position:** hybrid. He loves traditional publishing and still uses it; indie gave
authors control they'd never had.

## Historical context, briefly

SF began in serialized pulp magazines — cheap, fast, disposable. Early contracts were vague and
often exploitative; writers signed whatever was in front of them with no transparency.

Golden Age authors (Asimov, Clarke, Bradbury) built reputations through short stories, then
pitched novels with credentials in hand. **The 1980s shift toward novel-length work eliminated
that pathway** — which is worth knowing, because a lot of older career advice assumes it still
exists.

His summary of the industry: a mishmash of creative people and business people who don't always
communicate well. Slow, weird, and among the most rewarding things you can do.
