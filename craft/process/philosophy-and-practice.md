## Advice is a toolkit, not a body of law

Writing advice is not universally right or wrong. A technique is useful when it solves a
specific problem, for a specific writer, on a specific project.

**Two successful writers give opposite advice because they:**
- have different processes
- write different kinds of stories
- struggle with different weaknesses
- are addressing writers at different stages
- use the same words to mean different things

**So the question is not** *is this advice true?*

**The question is:**

> What problem is this advice designed to solve, and do I currently have that problem?

### How to adopt a technique

1. Identify the problem it addresses
2. Test it on a scene, chapter, or short project
3. Observe whether it improved the work *or the process*
4. Keep, modify, postpone, or discard
5. **Reconsider discarded tools later** — a technique that's harmful at one skill level can
   become useful at another

**The common mistake:** turning a successful author's personal process into a universal
commandment.

## Chef, not cook

| | |
|---|---|
| **Cook** | Follows recipes. Helpless when something goes wrong |
| **Chef** | Understands why the recipe works. Can invent and troubleshoot |

> I want to treat you like a chef, not a cook.

A recipe-following writer memorizes rules. A craft-oriented writer understands **the reader
response each technique produces.**

The goal of studying craft is not obedience to formula. It's understanding mechanics well enough
to diagnose a broken scene, produce a specific emotional effect on purpose, adapt to unusual
stories, and break conventions without accidentally breaking the reader's experience.

### The diagnostic habit — **[not from the lectures]**

> **Not Sanderson's.** Verified against the Lecture 1 transcript: this checklist does not appear.
> It came from an earlier working summary and is retained as this project's own tool because it
> works — but do not attribute it to him.

Whenever you learn a rule, ask:

1. **What reader expectation does this create?**
2. **What emotional response is it trying to produce?**
3. **What usually goes wrong without it?**
4. **Under what circumstances would breaking it improve the story?**
5. **What would I need to provide instead?**

This is the single most useful meta-tool here. It converts every craft document from an
instruction into an object you can reason about — which is the difference between the two modes
above.

## Writing the book teaches more than studying the book

> You will learn more by writing your first book than I or any person can possibly teach you.

Craft education shortens the curve. It does not replace completing stories.

**A first novel forces problems isolated exercises can't reproduce:** sustaining arcs across many
chapters, maintaining momentum, handling setup and payoff, finding out which planning methods
actually help *you*, finishing after the excitement fades, and revising a whole structure rather
than polishing fragments.

**So the first objective is completion, not brilliance.**

### The warning sign

**Repeatedly rewriting the opening chapters.** It feels like serious work and it prevents you
from ever learning endings, escalation, payoff, or full-book revision. You can spend years
producing an excellent chapter one and no novelist.

### The practical rule

Unless the draft has become genuinely unusable: **record the problem in a revision document and
keep going forward.**

## Find your position between outliner and discovery writer

**Architect** (planner) and **gardener** (discovery writer) are not identities. Most writers mix
both, and the ratio shifts by project, by subplot, by experience, and by drafting stage.

One writer outlines plot mechanics and discovers character relationships in prose. Another
discovers a first draft and heavily structures the second.

**His proof it doesn't matter which:** he endorses outlines, Stephen King rejects them, both
work.

### The experiment worth running

Across three projects or sequences, try:

1. A detailed outline
2. A loose list of major destinations
3. Minimal planning — premise and a character desire

Compare: drafting speed, enthusiasm, dead ends, revision load, quality of endings, sense of
spontaneity, ability to foreshadow.

> Don't choose a label because it sounds artistic. Choose a process because it produces work you
> can finish and revise.

## Know your revision bias

**This follows directly from the above, and it's the most practically useful thing in this
document.** Your drafting style predicts your revision failure mode.

### Outliners tend to revise too little

The structure feels coherent because it was coherent in the outline — so problems in the actual
*reader experience* get underestimated.

**Watch for:**
- Scenes that technically perform a function but feel lifeless
- Characters behaving according to plan rather than psychology
- Emotional transitions assumed rather than dramatized
- Not enough discovery happening during drafting

### Discovery writers tend to revise too much

The story is found by writing it, so early material gets rewritten repeatedly before the whole
thing is understood.

**Watch for:**
- Endless opening revisions
- Resistance to finishing
- Attachment to exploratory scenes that no longer serve the book
- Perfecting prose before the structure is stable

### The correction

**Outliners:** test emotional execution, not structural soundness.
**Discovery writers:** delay major reconstruction until you know what the story became.

## The real product of writing time is the writer

A manuscript may fail, stall, or be replaced. **The skills stay.**

Writing builds observation, empathy, patience, concentration, discipline, emotional
articulation, structural thinking, tolerance for uncertainty, and the ability to revise
imperfect work.

This reframes "failed" projects. A manuscript is not worthless because it didn't get published.

### Judge every session on two levels

1. **Project progress** — did the manuscript move?
2. **Writer development** — what did this session teach or strengthen?

## Separate the artist from the businessperson

Drafting and evaluation need different mental postures, and they must not share a session.

| Artist mode | Business / editor mode |
|---|---|
| Explores | Evaluates audience expectation |
| Takes emotional risks | Identifies weaknesses |
| Tolerates imperfect language | Considers positioning and length |
| Follows curiosity | Prioritizes revisions |
| Produces before judging | Decides against the project's goals |

**The method:** declare which mode you're in before the session starts.

- *Today I am generating possibilities.*
- *Today I am testing whether Chapter 4 advances the central conflict.*
- *Today I am cutting material that doesn't serve the intended book.*

**Trying to draft and prosecute the draft at once produces paralysis.** His long-term framing:
the two eventually work together — a buddy-cop dynamic, with revision instead of car chases.

## Build a repeatable process

Professional reliability depends less on inspiration than on knowing how to produce under
ordinary conditions.

**A process specifies:** when you write, where, how you begin, how you track, how you handle
missed days, how you get through difficult sections, how you revise, how you finish.

### Track what you control

Minutes spent, words drafted, scenes revised, focused sessions completed.

> Don't let an ambitious tracking system become a second hobby that replaces writing.

### Goal design

A useful daily goal is measurable, achievable on a normal day, demanding enough to require
effort, flexible enough to survive interruption, and **judged over time rather than by one bad
day.**

### His own numbers, as calibration not target

**Source: 2020 · SECONDARY**

- 2,000–3,000 words/day across two four-hour sessions
- 300–700 words/hour is a normal range
- **A 100,000-word novel is achievable at four hours a week over a year**

That last figure is the one to keep. Most people who believe they have no time to write a novel
have four hours a week.

### Protecting the time

- Set boundaries — his: no work 5:30–9:00 PM
- **A 5–15 minute interruption can cost 45 minutes** of flow. Interruptions aren't proportional
  to their length
- Enlist the people around you as guardians of the time rather than competitors for it

### Choosing a day job

- **Menial labor is good** — the mind stays free to plot
- **High cognitive demand is bad** — programming, teaching. Same resource writing needs
- Graveyard shifts work; 11 PM–5 AM is viable

## Design systems for the parts you dislike

Writers build routines around what they enjoy and rely on willpower for everything else. That's
how manuscripts go unfinished and unrevised.

For a disliked task:

1. Identify the exact point of resistance
2. Reduce it to a visible next action
3. Pair it with an established habit or a pleasant condition
4. Track completion
5. **Work a prioritized list instead of confronting the whole manuscript**

### The revision queue — order matters

Don't sit down to *fix the novel*. Work a queue, structural first:

1. Repair the protagonist's motivation
2. Remove or combine redundant scenes
3. Strengthen the midpoint reversal
4. Seed information the climax requires
5. **Line-level prose only after structural repairs**

That ordering is the point. Polishing sentences in a chapter with a motivation problem is wasted
work — see [revision-and-diagnosis.md](../revision/revision-and-diagnosis.md).

## Professional ambition is optional; serious practice isn't

Society interrogates writers about publication in a way it doesn't interrogate hobbyists in any
other domain. Nobody asks a weekend guitarist when they're going on tour.

> It's okay to be a non-professional writer. Writing is good for you. It's good for your soul.

But anyone pursuing a career should be realistic: it requires repeatable execution, deadlines,
revision, persistence, and the ability to produce *more* work after both rejection and success.

**Early projects should be used to** discover a sustainable process, learn to finish, build a
body of work, practice revision, increase consistency, and develop resilience.

Publication pressure introduced too early distorts that learning phase.

### Expect several novels first

Roughly **three to five completed novels** as a process-discovery period. Not a magic threshold —
the point is that novel writing is many separate skills, and one attempt isn't enough repetition.

**Skills that need repeated full projects:** openings, middles, endings, escalation, character
arcs, viewpoint control, foreshadowing, revision, whole-book pacing, and judging which ideas can
sustain novel length.

**Replace** *my first novel must prove I can be an author*
**with** *my current novel must teach me something that improves the next one.*

### The odds

**Roughly 1 in 20**, from his teaching experience — not one in a million. Competitive, not
lottery-like. Zero without an attempt; considerably better with sustained effort.

## Ideas are cheap; execution creates value

> Authors make the ideas work.

A bad idea given to a good writer produces a great book. A good idea given to an unpracticed
writer fails. Distinctive fiction comes from execution — character choices, consequences, scene
construction, voice, specificity, emotional development, thematic pressure, setup and payoff.

**Waiting for a flawless idea is a form of avoidance.**

**And:** using a beloved idea early does not permanently spend it. You can come back to it later
with more skill and rebuild it.
