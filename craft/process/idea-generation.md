# Idea Generation

**Source:** Sanderson, 2020 BYU course, Lecture 2 · **Tier:** SECONDARY
([Nicole van der Hoeven's notes](https://notes.nicolevanderhoeven.com/sources/Course/Brandon+Sanderson+on+Writing+BYU+2020), [community notes](https://gist.github.com/jake9696/86f1f9d65af275d77435f6b33c78d64b))
**Reach for this when:** starting a new project, or a premise feels thin or generic.

---

## Atomic ideas

**His method:** keep a notebook of **atomic ideas** — single, small, uncombined fragments. Then
look for combinations that produce something interesting.

Atomic ideas are not premises. They're the raw material:

- A visual: cathedral lights in mist
- A structural notion: an *Ocean's Eleven* heist crew, in a fantasy world
- A question, an image, a rule, a character shape

**The point of keeping them atomic:** a fragment can combine with anything. A fully-formed
premise can't combine with anything, because it's already committed.

This is a Zettelkasten-style practice — value comes from the collisions, not the individual
notes.

---

## The strange attractor

**Combine familiar ideas in unexpected ways**, so the story feels familiar and strange at once.

The shorthand: *it's this meets this.*

**His own worked example:** heist + epic fantasy + *My Fair Lady* master/apprentice = *Mistborn*.

**Why it works:** the familiar half gives the reader footing; the strange half gives them a
reason. Pure novelty is disorienting. Pure familiarity is a book they've read.

**Where it connects:** plot is the hardest pillar to innovate on, and setting is the easiest.
The strange attractor lets you get originality out of *combination* rather than out of inventing
a new plot — which is the cheaper and more reliable route.

See [../plot/plot-archetypes.md](../plot/plot-archetypes.md) — stripping a plot to its archetype
and re-dressing it in different genre trappings is the same move.

## The three-pillar readiness check

**Source: [Lecture 8, 2025](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025) · PRIMARY**

A novel comes together where strong ideas converge across **setting, character, and plot.** His
threshold: **a couple of good ideas in each area** before it feels like a novel.

**Use this as a gate.** One brilliant idea in one pillar is a short story at best, and more often
it's an idea you're in love with rather than a book.

## Developing an idea: the consequential questions

**Source: prior working synthesis (SYNTHESIS)**

Combining two things you care about produces a premise. **A premise is not a story.** To find out
whether it can carry one, ask what it makes possible and impossible:

1. **What does the premise allow the protagonist to do?**
2. **What does it prevent them from doing?**
3. **Who benefits from this situation?**
4. **Who is harmed?**
5. **What difficult choice is unique to this premise?**
6. **What promise does the premise make to the reader?**

Question 5 is the test. If the premise doesn't generate a dilemma that *couldn't happen in
another story*, you have a setting rather than a novel.

Question 6 hands you straight to [../plot/promises.md](../plot/promises.md) — the premise is
already promising something, and you should know what before you write the opening.

## Ideas are not the constraint

From [philosophy-and-practice.md](philosophy-and-practice.md): authors make ideas work. A bad
idea given to a good writer produces a great book.

**Therefore:** stop hoarding, stop protecting, stop waiting for a better one. The ideas notebook
exists to give you combinations, not to store treasures.

**And the reassurance that removes the last excuse:** using a beloved idea early does not
permanently spend it. You can return to it later with more skill and rebuild it. The idea you're
saving for when you're good enough is an idea you're not learning from.

## Checks

- [ ] Do you keep atomic ideas somewhere, separate from developed premises?
- [ ] What two things is this project combining? Can you state it as *this meets this*?
- [ ] Is the combination familiar-and-strange, or just one of the two?
- [ ] Do you have two solid ideas in each of the three pillars?
- [ ] Are you innovating on plot when you could innovate on setting or combination instead?

## Failure modes

- **Premise hoarding.** Storing finished concepts instead of combinable fragments.
- **Pure novelty.** Strange with nothing familiar to grip.
- **Pure familiarity.** Competent and forgettable.
- **One-pillar projects.** A great magic system with no characters and no plot.
- **Waiting for the good idea.** The idea was never the constraint.
