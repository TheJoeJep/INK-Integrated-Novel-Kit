# Feedback and Critique

**Source:** Sanderson, [The Philosophy of Professional Writing, Lecture 1 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-writing-class-2025-week-1) (PRIMARY) and a [prior working synthesis](../SOURCES.md) drawing on the lecture video (SYNTHESIS)
**Reach for this when:** joining or running a writing group, giving critique, or deciding when to
show work.

---

## A writing group is a tool, not a requirement

A useful group needs compatible expectations about:

- submission length
- meeting frequency
- genre
- seriousness
- critique style
- time commitment
- confidentiality
- **whether members want encouragement, diagnosis, or intensive criticism**

That last one is the usual hidden mismatch. Two people can both be acting in good faith and
still be useless to each other because one came for morale and the other came for a scalpel.

Groups fail on misaligned expectations, not on mismatched talent. His own college writing group
is the example of it working when properly aligned.

**What groups actually provide** (2020, SECONDARY): deadlines, support, and feedback structure.
The deadline is often the most valuable part.

---

## Receive feedback without defending the draft

> Learn to take feedback without defending yourself.

**Why:** the moment you explain what you intended, the reader starts responding to your
explanation instead of your manuscript. You've contaminated the sample. No reader will be
standing next to your book explaining it for you.

**The rule:** don't talk during critique. Take notes. Ask clarifying questions afterward.

This is the hardest discipline in the process and it determines whether the group is worth
attending at all.

### What to record

- Where readers became confused
- Where attention weakened
- What they expected
- What they believed characters wanted
- Which moments felt emotionally strong
- **Where multiple readers independently reacted the same way**

That last one is the signal. One reader's reaction is data; three readers' identical reaction is
a diagnosis waiting to be made.

## The three tiers of what a reader gives you

**The most useful distinction in this document.** Readers hand you three different things and
they have wildly different authority:

| Tier | Authority | Treat as |
|---|---|---|
| **Reader reaction** | High — they are the world expert on their own experience | Evidence |
| **Reader diagnosis** | Medium — a plausible interpretation | Hypothesis |
| **Reader prescription** | Low — one option among many | Optional suggestion |

> The reader is generally more authoritative about their reaction than about the best repair.

**In practice:** *"I got bored in chapter nine"* is near-certain. *"Because there's not enough
action"* is a guess. *"You should add a fight scene"* is one of twenty possible fixes and
probably not the best one.

Take the location seriously. Diagnose it yourself with
[../reference/problem-to-technique.md](../reference/problem-to-technique.md).

**Related, from the character material:** readers *feel* structural problems and attribute them
to character. "I don't like the protagonist" is frequently a proactivity or motivation problem
underneath.

## Give descriptive critique, not prescriptive

**When you're the one critiquing:** your job is not to rewrite the story into the version you'd
have written.

**Weak:**
> The protagonist should leave the city in Chapter 3.

**Strong:**
> By Chapter 3, I felt the protagonist was waiting for events rather than pursuing anything, and
> my attention started to drop.

The second gives the writer accurate information *and* leaves the creative decision where it
belongs.

### Useful critique language

- "I expected…"
- "I became confused when…"
- "My attention weakened during…"
- "I interpreted this as…"
- "I wanted to know more about…"
- "This moment changed how I viewed…"
- "I didn't believe the choice because…"

Every one of these reports an experience rather than issuing an instruction.

## When to show work

**The danger:** getting critique before you've told yourself the story. Advice from several
readers can redirect an unfinished manuscript before the writer understands what it wants to
become.

| Writer type | When |
|---|---|
| **Outliner / architect** | Can handle earlier feedback — structure is already decided |
| **Discovery writer / gardener** | **Delay until the draft is done** |

**For discovery writers specifically, feedback is more useful:**
- after a complete draft
- on short fiction
- on a *previous* project while drafting the next
- **on specific questions rather than on the whole unfinished concept**

That last option is the underused one. "Does chapter four's ending make you want to continue?" is
answerable without inviting anyone to redesign your book.

## Expert readers for unfamiliar material

From [../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md](../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md):

- Read **primary sources** first — first-person accounts of what media gets wrong
- Get readers with direct experience
- **Three or four, not one** — people are individuals
- His *Skyward* example: actual fighter pilots substantially improved the book

**His finding:** every change made from this feedback improved the story **for all readers**, not
only for the group consulted.

## Checks

- [ ] Does your group agree on frequency, commitment, and **critique intensity**?
- [ ] Can you sit through a critique without explaining yourself?
- [ ] Are you a discovery writer sharing too early?
- [ ] Are you sorting feedback into reaction / diagnosis / prescription?
- [ ] Where did multiple readers react the same way independently?
- [ ] When critiquing others — are you describing your experience or rewriting their book?
- [ ] For unfamiliar material — three or four expert readers, not one?

## Failure modes

- **Defending during critique.** Destroys the session's value.
- **Early feedback on discovery-written work.** The most expensive mistake here.
- **Taking prescriptions literally.** Readers locate problems; they rarely diagnose them.
- **Prescriptive critique.** Rewriting someone else's book instead of reporting your experience.
- **A single sensitivity reader.** One person's experience treated as a category's.
- **Unstated intensity mismatch.** Someone wanted encouragement; someone brought a scalpel.
