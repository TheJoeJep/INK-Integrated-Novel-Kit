# Revision and Diagnosis

**Source:** Sanderson, 2020 Lecture 8 (the five-stage framework) and Lecture 4 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
Plus 2025 Lectures 1, 2 and 4.
**Reach for this when:** you have a draft and something is wrong — or you have a draft and don't
know where to start.

---

## The five-stage framework

**The most systematic revision material in the entire corpus.** Nothing in the 2025 course
approaches it.

### 1.0 — the straight-through draft

Beginning to end. Focus on **main plot arcs, characters' emotional beats, and a working set of
promises and payoffs.**

**Prose quality is explicitly not a concern.**

**The placeholder technique:** foreshadowing gaps get a note — *remember a bucket* — rather than
being fixed inline. **Momentum is the priority**, and fixing foreshadowing before you know the
ending is wasted work anyway.

### 2.0 — self-revision from a revision map

After finishing 1.0, build a **hierarchical to-do list** from your own drafting notes, organized
by scope:

| Tier | Contains |
|---|---|
| **Top** | Pervasive, book-spanning problems too large to spot-check — his example: discovering an entire character arc doesn't work |
| **Middle** | Scene- and sequence-level fixes |
| **Bottom** | Local, checkable items |

**Work top tier first.** A book-spanning problem invalidates fixes made below it.

### Alpha reads → a new revision guide

**Alpha readers** are industry-savvy people who can **diagnose** problems and often suggest fixes.

Their feedback becomes a **fresh prioritized list**, structured the same top/middle/bottom way.
Unresolved items from 2.0 carry forward onto it.

### 3.0 — incorporating alpha and editorial feedback

**The technique worth stealing:** test a suggested fix **in a single chapter first.** Gauge whether
it improves the book. Only propagate it if it does.

Rather than applying editorial suggestions wholesale on faith.

### Beta reads

**Beta readers** are casual readers and superfans standing in for the actual audience — as opposed
to alpha readers' industry judgment.

> **The goal here is *reaction*, not fixes.**

He wants emotional response data. His example: is this character driving every reader up the wall
— and if so, is that acceptable collateral for a minority of the audience, or a real
miscalculation?

**The purpose is not being surprised by audience reaction on release.**

### 4.0 — changes driven by beta reaction

**His precedent, attributed to Lucas:** an early cut of *Return of the Jedi* destroyed the
Millennium Falcon at the climax. A bad test-audience reaction led to cutting the beat before
release.

That's the model — emotional data from readers changes the draft.

### 5.0 — polish

The final pass, and note what's in it:

- **Cut roughly 10% of the length**
- Eliminate passive voice
- Upgrade weak verbs to strong ones

**Sentence-level craft is explicitly deferred to last.** This is the draft that goes to copyedit.

## Alpha vs. beta — the distinction that makes feedback usable

| | Who | What you want from them |
|---|---|---|
| **Alpha** | Industry-savvy; can diagnose and often prescribe | **Diagnosis** |
| **Beta** | Casual readers, superfans, your actual audience | **Reaction** |

**Asking the wrong one for the wrong thing is why feedback rounds go badly.** A casual reader's
prescription is a guess; an industry reader's emotional reaction isn't your audience's.

See [../process/feedback-and-critique.md](../process/feedback-and-critique.md).

## For newer writers: finish it

> Finish the story regardless of how broken you think it is.

**The act of finishing gives you the tools to fix it.** You cannot revise what you abandoned, and
one completed bad novel teaches more than five abandoned good openings.

## Diagnose systematically

Don't revise by feel. Break the problem into fundamentals:

1. **Motivation** — where are my characters' motivations? (~60% of character problems)
2. **Promises** — what did I promise, and did I pay it?
3. **Plot archetype** — what shape is this meant to be? Is it that shape?
4. **Pacing** — where does progress become invisible?

Full symptom mapping: [../reference/problem-to-technique.md](../reference/problem-to-technique.md).

## The split personality

Two selves, never in the room together:

- **Creation** — the artist directs. Don't self-edit while drafting
- **Revision** — the business-minded self takes over

**Editing while drafting is the most common way a first novel dies.**

**Revision bias by drafting style** *(partially verified — the pattern is his; the itemized
warning signs were an earlier draft's addition)*: outliners tend to skip second drafts because the
structure felt sound in the outline; discovery writers tend to loop on early chapters and stall.

---

## Foreshadowing belongs in revision

**Don't try to land foreshadowing in draft one.** Fix it in revision, when you know what you're
foreshadowing. Hence the *remember a bucket* placeholder.

For discovery writers this is close to a superpower: write the twist, then go back and make it
inevitable.

---

## Revising for originality

**Don't fear being unoriginal.** When a plot feels derivative, **try inverting the character
expectation rather than changing the plot.** He flipped *The Way of Kings*' protagonist arc on its
head and rewrote until it felt fresh.

Plot is the hardest pillar to innovate on; character is easier and does the same job.

---

## Adding late is legitimate

Adolin was added as a viewpoint **late in revision**, alongside Dalinar — the diagnosis being that
Dalinar was reading as an incoherent single character. It grew the book ~5% and he judged it worth
it.

**Adding a viewpoint in revision is a structural fix, not an admission of failure.**

## Checks

Before revising:
- [ ] Is the artist locked away, or are you still defending drafting choices?
- [ ] Do you have a **revision map**, tiered top/middle/bottom?
- [ ] Are you starting at the **top tier**?

During:
- [ ] Testing each suggested fix in one chapter before propagating it?
- [ ] Are you asking alphas for diagnosis and betas for reaction — not the reverse?
- [ ] Every promise checked for its payoff?
- [ ] Every character's motivation checked against their actions?
- [ ] Where is progress invisible for more than two chapters?

Polish, last:
- [ ] Cut ~10%
- [ ] Passive voice
- [ ] Weak verbs

## Failure modes

- **Editing while drafting.** Kills more first novels than anything else
- **Revising by feel.** Polishing sentences in a chapter with a structural problem
- **Working the bottom tier first.** A book-spanning fix invalidates everything below it
- **Applying editorial notes wholesale.** Test in one chapter first
- **Asking betas to diagnose.** They report reaction reliably and causes badly
- **Foreshadowing in draft one.** Wasted effort
- **Abandoning instead of finishing.** You lose the lesson
