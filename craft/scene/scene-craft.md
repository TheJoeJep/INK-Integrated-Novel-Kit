# Scene Craft, Plot Holes, and Hanging a Lantern

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lectures 2 and 3 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** writing or revising a scene, or a reader has snagged on something.

---

## What every scene owes

A scene must progress **something** — plot, character understanding, or the underlying conflict.
One is enough. Zero is where readers stop.

And every obstacle in it should **force, reinforce, or show failure in growth** — see
[../structure/structure-models.md](../structure/structure-models.md). An obstacle that does none
of those is an event, not a story beat.

## The stealth thesis paragraph

Open a chapter by establishing what it's about and what you'll learn; close on a **stinger line**
indicating you've learned it. Orients the reader without announcing itself.

## Ending a scene

**Yes-but** or **no-and**. Plain *yes* stops the engine; plain *no* stalls it. See
[../structure/try-fail-cycles.md](../structure/try-fail-cycles.md).

## The cold open

Open mid-action with a sequence that is **your story in microcosm** — *Raiders of the Lost Ark*
does fifteen pages of what the film then does in a hundred and fifty, ending in Indy losing.

Makes tone, character, and structural promises at once. Must be *representative*, or you've
promised a book you aren't writing.

# Plot holes

## The reframe

> A story cannot lack plot holes by those definitions, because a story cannot accurately
> represent real life without infinite space and time to tell it. Every story cuts corners.

He is openly critical of the Cinema Sins style of criticism, and the reason is useful:

> There's usually one actual plot hole, and the rest are answers that could be given in one
> sentence — or half the time, *were* given, and got ignored.

## The only test that matters

**Does it kick the majority of readers out of the story?**

Not *is it logically airtight*. Not *could someone construct an objection three years later*. The
question is whether readers, while reading, stop following the story to argue with it.

**His example:** how many people reading *The Lord of the Rings* stop to worry about the Eagles?
It never bothered him. It clearly bothers some people. **If a significant number of your beta
readers snag on something, that's your signal** — not a forum thread.

**And the fix is available:** sometimes your first try-fail cycle exists purely to retire a
plot-hole question. Have someone try the obvious thing and fail. See
[../structure/try-fail-cycles.md](../structure/try-fail-cycles.md).

# The gorilla in the phone booth

**His college friend's metaphor**, and the most useful diagnostic name in the craft base.

A character is walking down the street on the phone with their partner. It's a breakup. It's
tense. And the narrative says: *he passed a gorilla in the phone booth.* And then keeps going.

The reader stops. They're no longer in the breakup. They're thinking **but what about the
gorilla.**

> You've dropped an out-of-context problem on the reader.

**A gorilla is anything that makes the reader stop following the story to ask a question you
haven't signalled you'll answer.** Naming it makes it findable — half the value is having a word
for the thing.

## Hanging a lantern

**The fix, and it comes from stage.** When part of a set was badly built and there was no time to
fix it, they'd hang a lantern on the bad part and have a character remark on it — *oh yeah, that
lantern's never worked.* Now the audience stops worrying about it.

Applied to prose, you have two options for any gorilla:

**1. Don't put it there.**

**2. Explain it in passing** — and get something for free:

> He passed the gorilla in the phone booth. His friend Charlie, home from his shift dancing with
> the sign on the corner.

The gorilla is explained. Charlie is introduced. The reader now suspects Charlie matters later.
**One clause did three jobs.**

**3. Acknowledge without explaining** — *he saw the gorilla in the phone booth, and thought: I'll
deal with that later.* This signals the author is in control and will return to it, which is all
the reader needs to relax.

**Use sparingly.** Overuse draws attention to details that didn't need it.

## Keeping the gorilla on purpose

Sometimes you *want* the reader asking. Four ways to do it without losing them:

- **Jargon.** In SF and fantasy you can drop an unexplained term — *we can solve that with the
  XYZ* — and readers accept that an explanation is coming. Often you never need one
- **Put nothing important next to it.** If it will genuinely pull the reader out, make sure the
  following passage isn't something they need to retain
- **Hang a partial lantern** — acknowledge it, defer it
- **Make it the promise.** *Guardians of the Galaxy* has a gorilla every few pages. That's the
  tone promise: this story is weird. Note it usually comes with a **Watson character** who asks
  for an explanation and doesn't get one

## Where worldbuilding goes

Through the character's eyes, so description also characterizes the observer. What a character
notices is characterization; what a narrator catalogues is an encyclopedia entry.

See [../prose/prose-and-exposition.md](../prose/prose-and-exposition.md).

## Checks

- [ ] What progresses here? Name it
- [ ] Does the obstacle force, reinforce, or show failure in growth?
- [ ] Does the scene open by establishing its subject?
- [ ] Does it end **yes-but** or **no-and**?
- [ ] **Any gorillas?** Anything that makes a reader stop to ask an unsignalled question
- [ ] For each one — remove, explain in passing, hang a lantern, or make it the promise?
- [ ] Are beta readers snagging in the same place? That's a real hole. Forum objections aren't
- [ ] Is there an obvious solution the reader is shouting that nobody has tried and failed at?

## Failure modes

- **The subject-less scene.** Never declares what it's about
- **The unhung gorilla.** A coincidence or oddity the book never acknowledges
- **Lantern overuse.** Now the reader watches the writer instead of the story
- **Chasing forum plot holes.** Optimizing for objections nobody had while reading
- **The unrepresentative cold open.** Exciting, and a lie about the book
