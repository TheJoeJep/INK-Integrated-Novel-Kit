# Progress and Signposting

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 2 — *Promise, Progress, Payoff* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** the middle sags, a reader says they got bored, or a sequence isn't
landing despite good material in it.

---

## The most important of the three

He says so directly, and adds two things that make it worth believing:

> Progress is in many ways the most important, and the least showy. It's the one people pay
> attention to least — but it's 90% of your book.

A page-turner is usually a story that understands progress and how to dole it out.

---

## You control progress absolutely

At any point in any story you could write *he stopped being stupid, so they got married, the
end.* Tolkien could have written *Bilbo fell through a wormhole, appeared in Mordor, dropped the
ring in.* You've read books where the ending felt roughly like that — that's a deus ex machina,
and it's a progress failure.

Equally: you could spend fifty pages in which one second passes. Describe the room, get
philosophical, run a flashback. It's possible. It's boring, and you've probably read something
close to it.

**Therefore:**

> Your job is not to make things progress. It is to give a sense of satisfying progression.

Those are different jobs. The second is the one readers experience.

---

## Two decisions

### 1. How fast

Match pacing to the story. A thriller — external obstacle, next obstacle, next obstacle — paces
completely differently from a story about a man wrestling with whether he can bring himself to
kill his uncle.

Also match it to your own natural style.

### 2. What kind of progress, and how to signpost it

**Match the type of progress to the kind of climax you're building.**

**Information progress.** Native to mystery, but any plot where characters lack information and
must find it, ending in a reveal. Measured in clues found, suspects eliminated. Signposted by
characters saying it aloud: *we're getting closer, one more piece.*

**Relationship progress.** Buddy cop and romantic comedy are the same structure — the difference
is a fist bump or a kiss. Start with a clear indication these people don't get along, show ways
they do, allow backslides, build to *these two are better together than apart*. The climax is
when they finally connect.

---

## Which thread is the soul of your story

**The most useful idea in the lecture, and the one most likely to fix a real book.**

Stories run several of these at once, and **identifying which one your ending actually depends on
is critical** — because that's the one your progress has to serve.

**His example, worth reading in full:** *Guardians of the Galaxy* is ostensibly an adventure —
get the MacGuffin, clear our names, stop the world ending. That's the Big P plot.

The real story is *can these people work together*. Almost everything that isn't an action scene
— and some that are — is relationship progress. And the climax is the protagonist, who refused to
take his dying mother's hand, taking the hands of his friends. **The MacGuffin doesn't beat the
villain. The power of friendship does.**

The hard part is telling the difference between **the trappings of your story** and the thread
that actually matters.

**Longer stories can have more than one soul.** You can run a whole arc about people becoming
friends, resolve it, and then go into act three and solve the MacGuffin problem. *Moana* does
something like this with Maui's return — he judges it handled a bit clunkily, but the intent is
right: give the character arc its own climax just before the main one.

## Why books get boring

> When a book is boring, or people say they got lost, usually the signposts of progress are not
> there.

**And the specific cause he names:**

> You gave someone a really interesting promise, and then you as a writer got interested in
> something else.

A large subplot that doesn't appear to advance the main story reads as a diversion, however good
it is.

### The worked example — *The Last Jedi*

Finn is established across two films as someone who cares about Rey and wants to find and help
her. Then the plot says: what if you freed some horses instead? What if it mattered that horses
should be free?

Finn is saying *my friend Rey, the fate of the universe, Luke Skywalker.* The plot is saying
*but the horses.*

A lot of viewers had exactly that experience — *why are we here, what are we doing.* Whatever
interesting thing was happening, they couldn't invest in it, because it didn't progress the
promises the story had made them.

### The worked example he uses against himself — *Oathbringer*

**This is the most instructive thing in the lecture, because it includes the fix.**

Characters are at point A. He knows they need to end up at point C. They're adrift in a new
environment, they decide to head for point B to get out, and then they get diverted, and
diverted again, and end up at C where he always needed them.

Readers were bored. Which shocked him, because he'd done extensive worldbuilding in that new
environment — the thing his books are known for.

> Really great worldbuilding cannot save a sequence where you've said people need to go to point
> B and then sent them to point C. The whole time, readers think *this is a side quest.*

**The fix, and it's small:** he gave the viewpoint protagonist a reason to want point C. Early
on, a source he trusts tells him that if he doesn't get to C, someone he loves will die. He
believes it completely.

Now the group is in conflict about where to go — and the reader is asking *are we supposed to go
to B or C?* rather than *why are we being diverted?* The reader now has a stake in reaching C.

Next round of readers: no complaints. Everything he'd wanted to be enjoyed was enjoyed.

**Note what he actually changed. He didn't change the route. He added a promise.** See
[promise-progress-payoff.md](promise-progress-payoff.md).

## Worldbuilding will not save you

Stated bluntly in this lecture, and it belongs here rather than in the worldbuilding docs:

> Worldbuilding is the least important of the three. Great worldbuilding with bad characters and
> bad plot is an encyclopedia.

Great characters and plot with weak worldbuilding is still often an excellent book. His example:
time-turners break the entire logic of the Harry Potter universe, and *Prisoner of Azkaban* is
still the strongest book in the series.

## Signposting techniques

**The stealth thesis paragraph.** Open a chapter by establishing what it's about and what you'll
learn; close with a stinger line indicating you've learned it.

**Say it aloud through character.** The detective who says *we're close*.

**Structural markers.** Chapter and part breaks.

---

## Pacing and variation

**Thriller pacing** — short chapters, a hook at the end of each, heavy external conflict. Dan
Brown is genuinely good at this and it's much of why *The Da Vinci Code* worked.

**His limit on it:** it runs out at around **70,000 words.** If you aren't heading into the
climax by then, you start losing people. So it works well for a 90–100,000 word book and is very
hard to sustain longer.

**Why you want slowdowns:** the reader needs a breather, the characters need one, and both need
room to process what's been discovered — so you can ramp back up.

> Variation is noticeable, and lack of variation creates a sense of coasting.
> If every sentence is a one-sentence zinger, then none of them are.

---

## Skipping time

If you need to move characters somewhere without spending the journey:

- **Make a mini-promise early** that the book will time-jump, then just do it
- **Use structural breaks.** End part one, begin part two with them arrived. A chapter break
  followed by *three weeks later* is far easier on a reader than the same jump mid-chapter
- **One instance establishes the pattern.** If chapter three opens *three days later*, you've
  set the convention
- You can also do it in a paragraph: *over the next three days she kept worrying — but at last
  they crested the hill and saw…*

Rowling does this constantly in Harry Potter. He thinks writers worry about it more than they
need to.

---

## Checks

Per chapter:
- [ ] Name the one thing that progressed
- [ ] Point at the line where the reader can *see* it
- [ ] Does it open by establishing its subject?
- [ ] Does it close with a stinger, or forward pressure?

Per sequence:
- [ ] Does the reader know why the characters are going where they're going?
- [ ] Could this read as a side quest? If so, whose want makes it not one?
- [ ] Are you relying on worldbuilding to carry a sequence with no progress?

Per book:
- [ ] Which thread is the **soul** — what must the ending resolve?
- [ ] Does the progress you're showing match that thread, or the trappings?
- [ ] Has the pacing varied, or is it uniform?
- [ ] If you're running thriller pacing — are you under ~70,000 words to the climax?
