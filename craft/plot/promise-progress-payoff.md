# Promise, Progress, Payoff

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 2 — *Promise, Progress, Payoff* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** starting a story, diagnosing one that has stalled, or checking whether
an ending will land.

---

## What plot is for

Two jobs:

1. **Facilitate conflict.** It's the primary way conflict gets made — something must happen,
   characters must do it, something stands in the way.
2. **Give the reader structure.** Something to hold onto; a sense that a story is being told and
   is moving.

And the framing that organizes everything else:

> **The intersection of plot, setting, and character is conflict.**

All three exist to create or facilitate conflict. That's what the story is about. A scene where
all three are present but none of them are pressing on each other is the most common kind of
dead scene.

## Don't innovate on plot

Ease of innovation, easiest to hardest: **setting → character → plot.**

Plot is hardest, and — this is the useful part — **it mostly doesn't need innovating.** Readers
expect certain things from a satisfying story, and most ways of subverting those expectations
just produce an unsatisfying story. You may want that. Usually you don't.

**His example, and it's a good one:** *Dune* is *Hamlet*. So is *The Lion King*. Son is wronged,
father dies, uncle seizes the throne, son goes away and comes back stronger (and crazier),
becomes a rival, kills the uncle, restores order. Both even skip the growing-up part.

Nobody reads *Dune* and *The Lion King* and thinks *those are the same story*. The shared
structure costs them nothing.

> Combine a familiar plot with interesting characters and an interesting setting and it will feel
> fresh and unique.

Spend your originality where it's cheap. See
[../process/idea-generation.md](../process/idea-generation.md).

## Big P and little p

**Big P Plot** — what the book is about. What you say when someone asks. *Lord of the Rings*:
destroy the ring.

**little p plot** — what happens chapter by chapter to keep the reader engaged. *There are orcs
in the way; we'll dress as orcs.* Small problem, resolve, small problem, resolve, building toward
larger ones.

**The trap this exposes:** your Big P and your little p can be about different things, and the
little p is often the real story. *Guardians of the Galaxy* is ostensibly *get the MacGuffin,
save the world.* The actual story is *can these people get along* — and the climax is people
taking each other's hands, not the MacGuffin. Identifying which thread is the **soul** of your
story determines what your ending has to resolve.

## These are diagnostic tools first

**A theme of his entire course, and worth taking seriously before applying any of this:**

Most writers don't draft while thinking *what is the progress here?* They write by instinct.
Then something doesn't work, and they back up and ask what the disconnect is.

> Writing will teach you how to do this better than any other activity.

So don't feel you must learn all of this up front and apply it to an outline before starting.
Use it to analyze what's broken. If you outline, it's useful there too.

### The most valuable single insight in the lecture

When promise and progress are mismatched, **he changes the promise, not the progress.**

> I go back and make new promises, because the progress I'm writing along is what I want to tell
> the story about.

This is backwards from most people's instinct, and it's usually right. The middle is where the
book actually lives, and it's where you discovered what you were interested in. Rewriting three
opening chapters to match it is cheap. Rewriting forty middle chapters to match an opening you
wrote before you understood the book is not.

**Which is also why promises are the easiest part.** You should not stress about them at the
beginning. Once you know your progress and have written an ending — a bad one, if you're a
discovery writer — nailing the promise is a three-chapter rewrite.

**He almost always throws away his first chapter, often his first several, and rewrites them
after finishing.** Even as an outliner.

**The corollary warning:** don't be the person who revises chapter one repeatedly until the book
is done. For most writers you can't get the promise right until you've gone far enough to know
what you're promising.

## Diagnosis

| Symptom | Usually |
|---|---|
| Readers quit early | Promise unclear, or the wrong promise |
| Readers quit in the middle | Progress not signposted — see [progress](progress-and-signposting.md) |
| Readers finish feeling cheated | Payoff answered a promise the book didn't make |
| A sequence is boring despite good material | **Promise/progress misalignment** — see below |

> Most of the time, if a book is boring, or people aren't investing in a sequence you thought
> they would, you have made a misalignment of progress and promise.

---

## A cultural caveat he raises himself

This is **modern Western storytelling tradition.** Asked directly how universal it is, he
answered that promise and progress travel well — *Journey to the West* runs on much the same
methodology — but **payoff is where traditions diverge most.** What satisfies differs, and
ambiguous endings carry different weight in different traditions.

He notes the two are homogenizing in both directions. Worth knowing that the framework is not
a law of nature.

## Red marks

His metaphor for what makes a book hard to get into:

Every book has red marks — things that would make someone put it down. **Every great book has a
lot of them.** Your job isn't to remove them all. It's to remove the ones **not integral** to the
art you're making, and then lean on the integral ones hard enough that they become what
distinguishes the book.

**His example against himself:** *The Way of Kings* has three prologues (one called a Prelude,
one a prologue, and chapter one is effectively a third). That's a terrible idea. The one-star
reviews all say the same thing — couldn't get through the first ten chapters, too many names,
Kaladin's too depressed.

And it buys him something no cleaner book could have: he can start very low and go very high,
and build a world so deep that when it clicks it's unlike anything else.

**The general rule he draws:** breaking the rules raises the threshold for grabbing readers, and
raises the ceiling once you do. Blandness gets fewer devoted followers. Formulaic romance gets
read and disposed of; the ones that break form are the ones that become enormous.

**The condition:** you have to earn it. Traditional fantasy wants ~120,000 words. *Way of Kings*
is 400,000. *Elantris* went out at 250,000 and came down to 200,000. That's allowed as long as
you're earning it.

## Checks

1. **What did this promise?** One sentence. If you can't say it, the reader can't either.
2. **Where was it promised?** Point at the page.
3. **What is the soul of this story** — which thread must the ending resolve?
4. **Since the last chapter, what progressed?** Name it.
5. **If progress and promise disagree — is the promise the thing to change?** Usually yes.
6. **Does the payoff answer the promise the book made?** Not a better one you thought of later.
7. **Which red marks are integral, and are you earning them?**
