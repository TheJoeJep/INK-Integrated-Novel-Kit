# Promises

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 2 — *Promise, Progress, Payoff* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** writing an opening, or diagnosing why readers bounced off one.

---

## The reassuring part

**Promises are the easy one.** Don't stress them at the start of a book.

Once you know your progress and have written an ending, throwing away three chapters and
rewriting so the promise nails what the story turned out to be is cheap. Throwing away forty
chapters in the middle is not.

He almost always discards his first chapter — often several — and rewrites after finishing.

**But don't confuse "easy to fix later" with "unimportant."** Promises are the easiest *and* the
most important, because they're where readers decide whether to continue.

---

## First lines vs first pages

First lines rarely matter as much as people say. *Call me Ishmael*, the *1984* clock striking
thirteen, the *Snow Crash* opening — great lines are worth having, and they are not what keeps
people reading.

**Your first few pages are where someone decides to put the book down.**

**The uncomfortable observation he adds:** most published books you've read are relying on
something before the first line — **the author's name.** An established writer trades on trust
you don't have yet. He can open *The Way of Kings* with three prologues because readers already
believe he'll get somewhere. A first novel gets no such credit.

---

## Tone promise

**What it is:** telling the reader what kind of story this is and how it will feel.

**How it's made — and it's more than the plot:**

- **Early jokes**, if the book is funny
- **An opening act of brutality** — a viewpoint character beheaded by someone who doesn't even
  want to kill him
- **Names.** The Stark family is a tone promise. Naming your wizard school Hogwarts and your
  Gandalf Dumbledore promises whimsy
- **Prose style** in the opening
- How you introduce things

### The epic fantasy prologue, explained

Worth understanding as a mechanism rather than a convention.

Epic fantasy often starts slow — a young nobody looking at the sky, wondering what's out there.
That's the *plot's* natural beginning, and it's **not the tone of the book.** The book's tone is
being chased by space wizards while things explode.

So: add a prologue whose entire job is the tone promise. Door blown off its hinges, laser fire,
a dark figure through the smoke. *Then* cut to the farm boy.

**His example:** *The Wheel of Time*. The book is about what happens when power costs you
everyone you love. The prologue shows a man with that power who has already gone mad and killed
everyone he loved.

**His caution:** this is now close to a cliché. Don't do it because it's done. Do it because your
story genuinely needs it — and know that if everyone's doing it, it's harder to stand out doing
it. He used it in *Stormlight* and deliberately didn't in *Mistborn*.

## Story / plot promise

**What it is:** a promise about where the whole thing is going.

**The problem:** sometimes your main conflict doesn't arrive until the end of act one — or
later. You can't promise a plot the characters haven't met.

**Three ways out:**

1. **Let the tone promise carry it.** You don't have to promise everything.
2. **Introduce a metaphor for the main conflict** — something in microcosm.
3. **Use the character promise instead**, and throw the Big P plot at them at the end of act one.

**When it's easy:** *Star Wars* has it immediately — here are the Death Star plans, we're going
to blow up the Death Star. Tone and plot promise both delivered in the cold open.

**When it isn't:** *Lord of the Rings*. What's the plot? You get a miniature version — gather
your friends, it's hard, reach Rivendell — and then you do it again over three books.

---

## Character promise

Three things:

1. **What do they want?**
2. **What do they need?**
3. **Why can't they have it?**

**His illustration:** a character trying to fix a broken-down hunk of junk. *Transformers* — I
need a car, cars are hard, I found a car — and the film is about cars. A small introduction to
the want and the obstacle.

Every character carries innate promises, usually attached to their arc — the flaws, handicaps,
and challenges you give them signal which will be overcome, which expunged, where they'll grow
and where they'll fail to.

---

## The cold open

**What it is:** a mini-story at the start that is **your story in microcosm.** Fifteen pages of
what you're then going to do in a hundred and fifty.

**His example, in full:** *Raiders of the Lost Ark*. Indiana Jones, cool adventurer, faces
trials, uses his wits and his whip to overcome them — **and at the end he loses.** The rest of
the film is the same shape at length.

**The heist version:** open with the crew pulling a job, so you learn how good they are. Then
either they replicate it or the next one goes wrong — and you know how it was supposed to go.

*Star Wars* opens in medias res for the same reason.

## Structural promises and series

Length is itself a promise — readers look at the size of a book and decide how long to give you
before it gets going.

**Series promises:**

- **The family structure.** Here is a family of seven unmarried eligible people; book one is the
  eldest. The reader knows it's a seven-book series from chapter one.
- **The named-series promise.** *The [X] Files* — these are the continuing adventures of this
  character, each book self-contained.
- **The long-arc promise.** *The Wheel of Time* — the Dragon Reborn will fight the Dark One at
  the end. Jordan pitched it as three books; the first was going to end with taking the sword
  that is not a sword. That's book three. As he puts it: this is what happens when discovery
  writers get series.
- **The packaging itself** promises structure once you're established.

## Making new promises mid-book

**Yes, and you should.** Every new character brings promises. Every plot sequence usually has
one. Every chapter generally has one.

**The chapter-level version:** the best chapters often open with a **stealth thesis paragraph**
that establishes what the chapter is about and what you'll learn, and close with a **stinger
line** indicating you've learned it.

**And the repair use:** a mid-book promise is the standard fix for a sequence that's dragging.
See the Oathbringer example in
[progress-and-signposting.md](progress-and-signposting.md).

## Switching tone deliberately

If the book alternates between two registers — thriller and travelogue, say — **promise both at
the beginning.**

**The rule-breaking option:** *Into the Woods* is the famous breaker. It promises idyllic
fairytales and then stomps them flat after the intermission. It works because of a twist
mechanism — it makes you stop wanting the fairytale ending before it takes it away. See
[payoff-and-twists.md](payoff-and-twists.md).

It also loses people permanently. His own mother won't forgive it.

---

## Short fiction

Everything compresses, but **it's not just shorter — it's more distilled.** The promise has to
land harder and earlier, often in the first paragraph. You can spend a whole prologue on a tone
promise in a fantasy novel. You cannot in a short story.

## Checks

- [ ] Tone demonstrated — through action, names, and prose — within the first pages
- [ ] Is there a story promise, or is the tone promise carrying it deliberately?
- [ ] Protagonist: want, need, and obstacle all visible
- [ ] Are you relying on a name-recognition hook you don't have?
- [ ] If using a tone-promise prologue — does the story need it, or is it habit?
- [ ] Does every chapter have a promise? Does it open with its subject?
- [ ] Are any two promises in conflict?
- [ ] Can the book actually pay everything promised here?
