# Payoff and Twists

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 2 — *Promise, Progress, Payoff* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** designing an ending, building a twist, or diagnosing an ending that
tested badly.

---

## The definition

> A payoff is a surprising yet inevitable fulfilment of your promises.

Both halves required. And note the emphasis in what follows: **you make it surprising, but you
rarely need a twist to do it.**

---

## Three ways to make a payoff surprising

### 1. Plausible deniability — the workhorse

**Make the reader believe it won't happen.** Most stories get enormous mileage from this, and
it's the technique to reach for first.

You're fairly sure Frodo succeeds and the world doesn't end. Then Shelob stings him and he
appears to die, and suddenly — *maybe Frodo won't. Maybe Sam has to do this alone.* That's
horrifying. Then you get Frodo back. Then Frodo collapses and Sam has to carry him.

**The three tools:** obstacles, escalations, and red herrings. Make people believe they won't
get what you promised, then give it to them anyway.

### 2. Over-delivery

You promised a toy car. You give a real car. This is escalation rather than a twist, and it's
often enough.

### 3. The twist proper

> Promise someone something, then make them wish you'd promised something else.

---

## The gift metaphor, in full

You tell someone: *you like toy cars? I'll get you a toy car.* You put a package under the tree.

- **The straight version:** they open a toy car. **Nothing is wrong with this.**
- **Over-delivery:** they open a real car.
- **The twist:** over the course of the story you show them how good planes are, until they're
  thinking *I'm getting a toy car, but I wish it were a plane.* Then they open a plane.

### *While You Were Sleeping* — the worked example

A woman watches a man pass through her tollbooth and imagines marrying him. He's in an accident;
she's there, she helps; through a misunderstanding everyone believes she's his fiancée. He wakes
with amnesia — *I'm glad my secret fiancée is Sandra Bullock.*

**Note that the amnesia is escalation, not the twist.** The twist is that across the whole film
she's been with his brother, and they genuinely fit. You were promised a toy car in scene one and
came to want the plane. She ends up with the brother.

He calls the signposting there deliberately unsubtle — and it works. You can do it more quietly,
so the twist lands as *this is what I always wanted.*

### *Into the Woods* — the gutsy version

Shows you idyllic fairytales, then puts cracks in them, so you begin not wanting the fairytale
ending. Then act two smashes everything — and rebuilds it into something better.

It's the mechanism working at full strength, and it costs him: his mother has never forgiven it.

## You do not need a twist

**The point he makes most emphatically.**

*Pride and Prejudice* — one of the greatest novels there is. The first line tells you this is a
book about someone getting married. Twist? No. **They get married.**

> There are complications and escalations, which are different from twists — and equally
> important, in fact more important.

---

## The Gandalf Promise

**The best technique in the lecture, and it needs no twist at all.**

Sometimes surprising-yet-inevitable comes from the reader **forgetting the promise.**

He compares the two Helm's-Deep-shaped sequences in the films:

**Minas Tirith (third film).** Up against all odds, this is their last stand, they fight, they
can't do it — and Aragorn arrives with the ghosts. His reaction: *good, the ghosts are here.* Not
a cheer.

> The ghosts did all the work. Everyone should have hidden and waited for the ghosts.

**Helm's Deep (second film).** Structurally identical — last stand, they'll die, someone arrives
and saves them. Except **Gandalf said *look for me on the morning of the fifth day.***

And Jackson does two things with it. He has Éomer's riders leave — so there's an army out there,
faithful until pushed too far, hanging on the mantle. And then he makes the fighting so dark, so
desperate, that Aragorn, Gimli and Legolas forget the promise, and so do you.

Then Gandalf arrives, exactly as promised, and you cheer.

> If you can do that, you don't need a twist. Make it so dark that when the light comes back, the
> reader cheers.

**The difference between the two sequences is entirely promise management.** Same event, same
structure, opposite effect.

*(His aside, which is a nice piece of craft thinking: he'd have preferred the third day, for
mythological reasons.)*

## Match the payoff to the soul of the story

Whatever thread is the actual soul of your book is the one the climax must resolve. See
[progress-and-signposting.md](progress-and-signposting.md) — *Guardians of the Galaxy* pays off
the friendship, not the MacGuffin.

Longer stories can resolve several. Give a character arc its own climax shortly before the main
one if it needs one.

## Bittersweet endings

**Source: 2025 Lecture 4.5 (Plot FAQ) · PRIMARY**

**Promise it early.** Make clear from the start that this is a world where things don't always
turn out as you'd want — life is hard here, friends have died before, there are casualties in the
middle of the book. A bittersweet ending arriving without that groundwork reads as a broken
promise.

### The rule that makes the balance predictable

**Which half the reader feels depends on which plot fails:**

| What happens | How it reads |
|---|---|
| Fails the **Big P** plot, but grows personally (little p) | **More sweet than bitter.** Readers are broadly fine with this |
| Achieves the **Big P**, but it costs them personally | **More bitter.** *Lord of the Rings* — the ring is destroyed and Frodo is destroyed as a person |
| **Dies** achieving the Big P, but completes their character arc | **Triumphant**, despite the death |

The films sharpen the LOTR case by cutting the Scouring, so you're left with the bare fact that
Frodo can't live a normal life. Triumphant, and very bitter.

**The practical read:** personal growth reads as the *sweet*. If your ending is landing more
bitter than you intended, the lever is usually the character arc, not the plot outcome.

**And calibrate on readers.** He watches beta readers specifically for this — how much bitter is
coming through, how much sweet, and whether the balance is what he wanted.

## Killing your darlings

Asked how he lets go of a plot or idea that felt core:

> You tell yourself a lie. *I will find a much better place for this where it can shine in its
> own story.*

Nine times out of ten you won't — because the idea wasn't actually that good, you just built the
story on it. The real creativity happens in the building, and by the time you're writing, the
thing that started it is no longer the best part.

**One time in ten it does become its own story**, which is what makes the lie survivable.

## Checks

- [ ] What promise does this answer? Is it the promise the book made?
- [ ] Surprising **and** inevitable?
- [ ] Where does the reader genuinely doubt? Name the scene
- [ ] Have you used obstacles, escalations, and red herrings — or only one?
- [ ] **Gandalf test:** could a kept promise, timed so they've forgotten it, beat the twist?
- [ ] If you have a twist — what did the reader want *instead*, and where was that built?
- [ ] Does the climax resolve the soul of the story, or the trappings?
- [ ] Are you protecting a darling because it started the book?

## Failure modes

- **The ghost army.** Rescue arrives; the reader is relieved rather than thrilled, because
  nothing promised it and nothing was paid for it
- **The unearned twist.** Hidden rather than redirected — the reader feels tricked
- **Twist as obligation.** Reaching for one when complications and escalations would serve better
- **Paying the wrong thread.** The MacGuffin resolves; the real story doesn't
- **No doubt anywhere.** Nothing to release
