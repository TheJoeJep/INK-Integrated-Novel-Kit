# Plot Archetypes

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 4.5 — *Plot FAQ* (PRIMARY, transcript), with 2020 Lecture 3
(SECONDARY) · **Reach for this when:** a subplot feels shapeless, or you want a combination
nobody's seen.

---

## The idea

A plot archetype is a **plot shape that produces a specific emotional experience.** Genre is the
trappings; the archetype is the machine underneath.

**The method he recommends:** watch or read a lot of them, **distil them into types and
subtypes**, and work out what you like about each. Not "heist" — *which kind* of heist.

**Scale rule:** the shorter the piece, the fewer archetypes it carries. Novels should generally
have several; a short story usually runs one.

---

## Two kinds of heist — and the distinction matters

He identified these while building *Mistborn*, and they behave completely differently:

### The Italian Job type
The crew works hard on a plan. At the end, **a giant disruption throws it all out.** They then
solve the problem using the resources and positioning they built up during the first half.

> None of that middle space is wasted — you're just using it again.

### The Ocean's Eleven type
They plot a genuinely difficult problem and then **execute it excellently.** Complications are
mostly *within* the plan. You're there to watch the plan unfold and enjoy how well it works.

**The technique that makes it work:** include a character who isn't fully in on the plan. Their
confusion creates tension in the climax — you think things are going wrong, and it was the plan
all along.

---

### The third kind — the bashing grab

**From the 2020 course, attributed to Joe Russo** discussing *Infinity War*: no elegant plan at
all. The crew simply goes in hard and takes it, and the story is the consequences.

Useful when the pleasure on offer is momentum rather than cleverness — and it's the shape a heist
collapses into when the plan fails badly enough.

## Three kinds of mystery

| | Engine |
|---|---|
| **Whodunit** | Work out who was behind it |
| **Cat and mouse** | The protagonist *is* the quarry, staying ahead of a pursuer |
| **The *Columbo* inversion** | **The reader knows from the start.** Tension comes from watching the net close, not from the answer |

The inversion is worth knowing because it removes the reveal entirely and still works — proof that
mystery tension isn't actually about withheld information.

**They can be stacked.** *The Fugitive* is a whodunit (who is the one-armed man?) with cat-and-
mouse on top — and the Big P plot is *can he stay ahead of Tommy Lee Jones.*

## Archetypes can transition mid-book

**One of the most useful things in this lecture.** You are not locked into the shape you started
with.

- ***Mistborn*** starts as a heist (Italian Job type) and, when the plan goes wrong, **becomes a
  revolution narrative.** The scope expands from *rob the world* to *save the world*
- ***Knives Out*** starts as a whodunit, reveals the answer at the midpoint, and **switches to
  cat-and-mouse** from the culprit's perspective

> Being able to modulate and switch between these is what a chef does.

---

## Every archetype can go sour

**A second lever most writers miss.** Any arc can be inverted:

- **An information arc** where the characters reach the **wrong** conclusion, and the problems
  come after
- **A relationship arc** that becomes a **betrayal arc**

### Writing a betrayal arc

His method, and it's specific:

1. **Run it as a relationship plot.** But instead of braiding the roses, have them keep stabbing
   each other
2. **You can skip the getting-to-know phase.** Open with them already close, and hit hard on why
   they work well together. Readers will coast on that a long way
3. **Show motivations diverging** — they keep running afoul of each other rather than helping
4. **Readers follow the viewpoint character's attention.** If the POV still thinks of them as a
   good friend, that *covers the cracks* — the reader will discount the warning signs too
5. **Make the reason for turning solid** — the reaction should be *I should have seen that*

> It's like any other twist. You're foreshadowing a relationship plot, and behind the scenes
> you're breaking it — so when it happens it's surprising yet inevitable.

## Common archetypes

| Archetype | Progress measured in |
|---|---|
| **Heist** (two kinds, above) | Sub-problems solved; the plan taking shape |
| **Master / apprentice** | Capability gained; the relationship shifting |
| **Whodunit** | Clues found, suspects eliminated |
| **Cat and mouse** | Distance kept, near-misses |
| **Relationship** | Emotional beats — or, inverted, a betrayal |
| **Underdog sports** | Wins, losses, training. See [../structure/genre-structure-mining.md](../structure/genre-structure-mining.md) |
| **Revolution** | Territory, allies, legitimacy |
| **Quest** | Distance, obstacles, cost |

## Finding combinations

> How do you find the combinations? Try them. **Swapping genres helps a lot.** Mixing and
> matching and just trying and seeing what works.

*Mistborn* is heist + master/apprentice + mystery + romance.

**And the permission that matters:** *Mistborn* is his **second attempt** — the first didn't
work.

> Sometimes you need to fail a few times. You need your own try-fail cycles before you can have
> your try-succeed cycles.

---

## Choosing which one carries the ending

Novels run several archetypes. Ask:

- Which one relates most to your **character arcs**?
- Which is your **Big P plot**?
- What are the big stakes?

The archetype carrying the soul of the story is the one the climax must resolve. See
[progress-and-signposting.md](progress-and-signposting.md).

## Checks

- [ ] What archetype is the main plot running? Each subplot?
- [ ] Have you picked a **subtype**, or just a broad category?
- [ ] Could this archetype productively **go sour** instead?
- [ ] Could it **transition** into a different archetype at the midpoint?
- [ ] Are you running too many for the length?
- [ ] Does the archetype's natural progress marker reach the reader?
- [ ] Which archetype does the climax resolve?
