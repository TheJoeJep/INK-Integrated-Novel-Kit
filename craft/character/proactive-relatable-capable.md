# Proactive, Relatable, Capable

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 5 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
Cross-checked against 2025 Lecture 3, and 2016 Lecture 2 for evolution.
**Reach for this when:** building any character, or diagnosing one readers don't engage with.

> His framing: of all his college writing classes, only **Dave Wolverton's** ever taught how to
> build a compelling character. He presents this as the thing the academy skips — and as *his*
> framework, one of many possible ways to teach it. The three words were chosen to rhyme.

---

## The framework

Three axes. Every character sits somewhere on each.

| Axis | Reader response |
|---|---|
| **Proactive** | "This person is making the story happen" |
| **Relatable** | "I understand this person" |
| **Capable** | "This person is good at something" |

**The shape to aim for:** very high on one, moderately low on another, and **growing on the
third** across the story. Decide which needs to grow, which is already a strength, and which
stays weak as a defining trait.

He notes he's never followed it one-to-one — it's a rule of thumb, not a formula.

### The axes are domain-specific, not personality traits

**Easy to miss and it changes how you use the framework.** His example: his wife is extremely
proactive about organizing family events or preparing a lesson — and would never volunteer to be
the one who saves the world. Same person, different position by situation.

So don't score a character globally. Score them *in the situation the story puts them in.*

## 1. Proactivity

> Probably the single thing that makes a character readable.

Readers bond to whoever moves the plot. They get frustrated by characters who stall it.

### The worked example — Jack in *Lost*

The pilot script **originally killed Jack at the end.** During casting the writers realized that
in all the plane-crash chaos, nobody mattered except him — because he was the one taking charge,
treating people, building the camp. So they rewrote it.

**And he's one of the least relatable leads in the show.** That's the point: a character can
carry a story on one axis alone.

### The villain problem

In many stories — origin stories especially — the villain is proactive and the hero is reactive.
The villain is out doing things; the protagonist would rather stay home. As he puts it: the call
to adventure, *but my pillow is so soft.*

**Villains don't even need to be sympathetic to become more interesting.** Sheer proactivity does
it. The *Dark Knight* cold open exists to establish the Joker's proactivity and capability at
once.

### Three fixes

1. **Accept it.** Proactivity isn't this character's trait — lean on relatability and capability
2. **Let the plot force it.** An act-one or midpoint turn from inactive to proactive
3. **Seed it small.** Show them proactive in a small personal sphere before the big plot

### Small-scale proactivity

It doesn't require saving the world. A character who turns back for one drowning stranger.
Mulan solving the chicken-feeding chore with a contraption. Small proactive problem-solving
primes us to want to watch them solve bigger problems.

### Proactivity under constraint

**Prison, injury, captivity.** *The Count of Monte Cristo* — a character who can't act externally
can still *try*: feigning illness, angling for extra bread, making plans. **The attempt carries
the signal**, even when it fails.

**The hardest case, which he names explicitly:** a character whose mental state makes it hard to
be proactive or to care about anything.

### The Mistborn rewrite — the most useful item here

A viewpoint character spirals into depression after a crisis of faith. The **first draft played
it static**, and readers struggled badly with those chapters.

**His fix:** he rewrote it so the character is actively working *point by point* through
remembered religions, trying to determine whether any might be true. Still sinking. But now
**making progress while sinking.**

> Motion is good, even if it's downward motion. Static is what's hard to write.

That sentence solves more manuscript problems than most of the framework around it.

### Restarting a stalled character

After a hard block — trauma at the end of act one, say. Two options:

- **Cut to another proactive character**, even within the same group, to carry momentum while
  this one sits with it. He does this in *Oathbringer*
- **Place the last growth beat late** — end of act two, start of act three — where something
  learned earlier resurfaces and prompts one final push

### Inaction vs. choosing the opposite

Watching someone drown and **choosing** not to help is still proactive — it's an active choice.
It costs you **relatability**, not proactivity.

**Frozen by fear is different.** That's a proactivity failure.

### Two proactive characters in opposition

Works well, and isn't confusing. *The Fugitive* — Ford and Tommy Lee Jones are both proactive and
both capable. The tension comes from Jones being *more* capable, which puts a clock on whether
Ford can stay ahead long enough.

## 2. Relatability

**Relatable does not mean likable.**

### Save the cat / kick the dog

Blake Snyder's title. Kindness makes a character likable instantly, regardless of their other
axes. Cruelty to something innocent flags a villain instantly.

**His example:** in Tad Williams' *Memory, Sorrow and Thorn*, a servant boy cares for a disabled
puppy — and a prince steps on it and kills it. That's the whole characterization.

### Seeing them through someone else's eyes

Self-narrated virtue reads as arrogance. Filtered through another viewpoint, it lands.

> We like what we like other people liking.

**His example:** chapter one of *The Way of Kings* is deliberately **not** Kaladin's viewpoint. He
wanted readers to see a larger-than-life heroic figure from outside first, then jump to Kaladin
as a broken slave in chapter two.

### Self-awareness

A character who half-knows they're behaving badly and makes clumsy efforts to improve earns
forgiveness — **as long as it isn't overplayed.** Darcy, who then puts his wealth and capability
to work for Elizabeth at the turn.

### Ordinary shared experience

Garfield hates Mondays and loves lasagna *because the audience does*, despite neither making
sense for a cat.

**His caution:** some "universal" traits aren't. *Struggles with math* lands for most readers,
not all. Broader framing — procrastination generally — reaches more people.

### Moral compass

A character whose morality roughly matches the reader's is more relatable, especially paired with
self-awareness about where they might take it too far.

### Villains, and the relatability nudge

Classic villains: very high proactive, very high capable, very low relatable.

> Nudging a villain even slightly up on relatability tends to produce the strongest villains.

**Magneto.** His goal — kill all humans — isn't relatable. His fear is: having survived a
concentration camp, he expects to lose everyone he loves the same way again.

*(He reads Rings of Power's handsome Sauron as an attempt at the same move, and says plainly he
doesn't know whether it worked.)*

### Downward relatability arcs

Some villains start relatively high and trend down. The Raimi *Spider-Man* villains nearly all
run tragic flaw → crosses a line → relatability collapses.

**The exception he flags: Sandman**, who stays comparatively relatable because his transformation
**happens to him** rather than being chosen. Worth keeping — chosen corruption and inflicted
corruption read completely differently.

### Relatability is relative

> The faster driver is a maniac; the slower driver is an idiot.

Your character reads as tolerable next to someone worse. This is the whole engine of antihero
design.

### Hero vs. protagonist

**Hero** = does virtuous things. **Protagonist** = the one we follow and want to succeed. Not the
same. He notes the lead of *Snow Crash* is literally named Hiro Protagonist as a wink at it.

### Antiheroes — two different things

**Classical / literary.** Refuses to fulfil the protagonist role, and the tension comes from
reader frustration at that refusal. Madame Bovary is *meant* to annoy you.

**Modern pop.** A villain who does heroic things. The recipe: highly proactive, highly capable,
low relatability — then set against people who rank even lower.

**John Wick is not a true antihero by his definition** — the killing is aimed entirely at worse
people. The mechanism is a **kick-the-dog moment that happens *to* him**: the puppy, a final gift
from his dying wife. That spikes sympathy and licenses everything after.

**A true antihero requires engaging the moral complexity** of what the character has done rather
than waving it away. Grimdark leans hardest into this — you're left with complicated feelings
rather than easy rooting interest. **Paul Atreides** is his example: arguably a villain, but the
hero's-journey shape keeps most readers from clocking it until too late.

### Tropes as a relatability shortcut

Let the reader file a character into a familiar trope, then show how they don't quite fit. Two
ways to break the box once it's set: **full reversal**, or **gradual expansion**.

## 3. Capability

**Readers will happily read whole books about competent people being competent.** Capability
alone can carry a story.

**Dirk Pitt** — "James Bond, if instead of being a womanizer and an alcoholic he were a Boy
Scout." The comic proof: a side chapter follows Pitt's *accountant*, alone and threatened, who
thinks *what would Dirk do* and steals a helicopter by sheer proximity to awesomeness.

**Too much capability suppresses relatability — but only matters if relatability is a goal for
that character.** In a story about someone being extremely good at things, that's the point.

**The correction case:** the *Casino Royale* reboot deliberately dialled Bond's competence back
and let him fail more, specifically to raise relatability against the near-infallible earlier
version.

**The standard pattern:** strong in one or two areas, weak elsewhere. Strengths move the plot;
weaknesses create tension.

**Capability isn't combat.** Sam Gamgee is routinely fans' favourite character in *Lord of the
Rings*, and his superpower — besides cooking — is loyalty absolute enough that it will not break.
Which is literally what saves the world.

## Flaw / restriction / limitation

| | |
|---|---|
| **Flaw** | Needs overcoming. The arc is built on fixing it |
| **Restriction** | **Self-imposed.** Makes things harder, and **you don't want it overcome** — Superman's moral code |
| **Limitation** | **Imposed against their will**, often physical. The story isn't about removing it but working around it — the one-armed athlete |

### A note on the names, because he changes them

He says in this lecture: *I've decided for this lecture series I'm going to call them* flaw /
restriction / limitation. **That's the settled 2025 terminology and the one to use.**

But he used **flaw / handicap / challenge** two lectures earlier, in Lecture 3, with the same
examples. And mid-Q&A here he forgets the word "restriction" entirely, fumbles for it, and jokes
that it's one of his flaws as a teacher.

| Course | Terms | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **2016 L2** | flaw / handicap | **Two categories only.** Physical conditions filed under *handicap* alongside self-imposed codes. Third axis called **likability** |
| **2020 L5** (Laws) | flaw / limitation / cost | Taught as part of the Second Law of Magic — applied to characters *and* magic |
| **2020 L9** | flaw / handicap / limitation | ⚠️ **"Handicap" here means close to the opposite of 2025's.** The concept 2020 calls *limitation* is what 2025 calls *handicap* |
| **2025 L3** | flaw / handicap / challenge | |
| **2025 L7** (Laws) | flaw / limitation / hindrance / cost | |
| **2025 L5** | **flaw / restriction / limitation** | **Settled.** *"I've decided for this lecture series I'm going to call them…"* ← use these |

**Six labellings. Learn the concepts, not the words:**

1. Something they **should overcome** — the arc is built on it
2. Something **self-imposed** that you *don't* want overcome — it reveals values
3. Something **inflicted**, not their fault, worked around rather than removed
4. *(In the magic lectures, a fourth:)* a **cost** paid per use

**Two things that are real developments rather than churn:**

- **2016 had no third category at all.** Separating *self-imposed* from *inflicted* is the
  substantive change
- **The magic and character versions are one idea.** In both Laws lectures he states the Second
  Law as a *character* principle first — Superman; why the story follows Frodo and not Gandalf —
  then applies it to magic systems. See
  [../worldbuilding/sandersons-laws-of-magic.md](../worldbuilding/sandersons-laws-of-magic.md)

**And one that's a correction, not a rename:** 2020 uses "likability" unexamined. 2025's central
claim is **relatable does not mean likable.** He sharpened that deliberately.

### A flaw becomes a liability if you harp on it

Relatable in small doses, grating if it blocks progress too long. **Luke wanting to go to Tosche
Station** works briefly; sustained resistance to the call would have curdled.

### The Michael problem — a named failure mode

*Lost* again. The character had strong ingredients: capability (he loves his son), a flaw (he was
a bad father), combining into a clean arc after the son is kidnapped.

The writers let it become his **only** trait in every scene — asked what he wants for lunch, he
answers *I want my son.* The fanbase disliked a character who should have been a slam dunk.

> A strong, well-motivated trait can still fail if it crowds out everything else.

## Motivation is the root cause

**When a reader says a character "just didn't work," it's a motivation problem roughly six times
out of ten.** Not dialogue. Not prose.

**Kurt Vonnegut, credited:** make a character want something from the start, even if it's just a
cup of coffee. *(Hudson Hawk appears to have taken this literally — Bruce Willis wants nothing but
a cup of coffee for the entire film, and disaster keeps preventing it.)*

**Characters should want multiple, sometimes contradictory things**, because people do — and as
the wants shift, the actions should shift legibly with them.

**Small wants evoke large ones.** Establishing Mulan's closeness with her father, small and early,
is what makes *I'll go to war in his place* land as earned.

**The failure case:** Finn in *The Last Jedi*. His established want — help Rey — is never bridged
to the film's actual ask, so his later sacrifice attempt reads as unmotivated.

See [motivation-personality-values.md](motivation-personality-values.md).

## Arcs

An arc is **movement up or down one of the three axes.**

- **Most common: up on relatability**, by visibly working on a flaw. The *working* raises
  relatability before it's resolved; resolving it is the moment the audience cheers
- **Second: up on capability** — Luke, from can't-use-the-Force to destroying the Death Star

Full treatment: [arcs-and-iconic-characters.md](arcs-and-iconic-characters.md).

## Checks

- [ ] Where does this character sit on each axis **in the situation the story puts them in**?
- [ ] Very high on one, moderate on another, growing on the third?
- [ ] Is any character maxed on all three?
- [ ] Is your antagonist out-proacting your protagonist? Which of the three fixes applies?
- [ ] For a stalled or trapped character — is there *motion*, even downward?
- [ ] What does this character want? Do they want contradictory things?
- [ ] Flaw, restriction, or limitation — which, and is the arc treating it correctly?
- [ ] Is one trait crowding out everything else? (Michael problem)
- [ ] For a villain — would nudging relatability up slightly make them stronger?
