# Character Voice, Quirks, and Dialogue

**Source:** Sanderson, [Customizing Your Character, Lecture 6 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/customizing-your-character-brandon-sandersons-writing-lecture-6-2025) · **Tier:** PRIMARY
2016/2020 dialogue material is a known [GAP](../SOURCES.md) — this is thinner than it should be.
**Reach for this when:** characters sound alike, or a character feels flat on the page despite
good design.

---

## Finding a character's voice

The goal: establish enough fundamentals that you can predict what they'd do in **any** situation.

### Revealing questions

His set — small, oblique, and effective precisely because they're not about the plot:

- What would this character do on a rainy day with nothing planned?
- **How do they treat service workers?**
- What's in their refrigerator right now?

### The Hollywood four questions

1. What does the character want?
2. Why can't they have it?
3. What do they **need**?
4. What must they sacrifice to get it?

The gap between 1 and 3 — want versus need — is where most arcs live.

### Core traits

Establish a few fundamentals so you can anticipate behavior. This creates consistency, and it
does something more valuable:

> If they ever break pattern, readers will notice and understand it's significant.

**A consistent character is a character whose deviations are meaningful.** Without the baseline
you can't spend the deviation.

### Visual inspiration

He mentions a friend who builds characters from magazine photographs — the visual cue triggers
the imagination. Worth knowing as an option if analytical methods stall.

## Dialogue

> Dialogue can do so much heavy lifting. This is probably the single most important tool for
> conveying personality.

### The foundational exercise

**Write a three-character scene with no dialogue tags and no setting description.** Make each
voice distinct enough that the reader never loses track of who's speaking.

This is the exercise. If you do one thing from this document, do this one.

### Three levers

**Dialect and accent.** Use sparingly. Heavy written dialect becomes grating — a few flourishes
go a long way.

**Speech patterns.**
- Short and clipped, or rambling?
- Do they interrupt themselves with new thoughts?
- Constant metaphors?

**Word choice.**
- Formal or casual?
- Technical jargon?
- Swearing patterns?
- Dated expressions?

### The intelligence stereotype — worth reading twice

The common error: writing intelligent characters by removing contractions and adding long words.

**His counterexample is specific and good.** His college roommate was Ken Jennings — the
*Jeopardy!* champion — and Ken doesn't use twenty-dollar words. He'll deploy one occasionally,
precisely, when it's exactly right. Not because it sounds smart.

**The better marker:** highly educated people **organize their thoughts differently.** Sentences
become more complex and build on each other. Get that into the diction and you evoke intelligence
with real depth.

## Quirks

> Characters will be remembered for their quirks first, and then you can add depth as you go on.

**The rule that determines whether a quirk works:** it must connect to a deeper trait, not exist
in isolation.

### His worked example — David from *The Reckoners*

David is terrible at metaphors. Why it works: it reinforces his core trait — he jumps into things
without thinking them through. That applies to metaphors too. He commits before considering what
the metaphor means, and his brain works oddly enough that the result is funny.

**The quirk is a symptom of the character.**

### His counterexample — and he names it as a failure

In *The Frugal Wizard's Guide to Medieval England*, a character rates everything five stars.
His own verdict: it didn't work as well, because it felt forced — it wasn't tied to the core
personality.

**Diagnostic:** name the deeper trait your quirk expresses. If you can't, it's decoration and
readers will feel the seam.

## Difficult character types

### Characters readers might dislike

Make them **interesting**, not likable. Clear motivations that make sense even when the actions
are objectionable.

**His caution about what readers actually won't forgive:** cruelty toward the protagonist,
especially cruelty that lands on their weaknesses. That's the line.

### Introspective or easily distracted characters

**Option 1 — external force.** Let plot momentum pull them along.

**Option 2 — make the voice so readable it doesn't matter.** Common in first-person romance:
the writing is interesting enough that you don't mind the lack of motion.

**Jane Austen** is his exemplar. It isn't even the first person — it's the voice of the prose and
the character. Readers stay for scenes that barely advance anything.

**Hitchhiker's Guide** is the extreme case: promise-progress-payoff is roughly seventh on the
list of what matters. The humor, the message, and the voice all outrank the mechanics.

**The principle:** voice can substitute for structure, but only if the voice is genuinely
exceptional. This is not a general-purpose exemption.

## Writing characters from backgrounds unlike yours

**1. Read primary sources.** First-person accounts — "here's what people get wrong about my
field," "here's what this experience is actually like." These tell you what media gets wrong,
which is the most valuable thing you can learn.

**2. Individuality over mold.** No real person fits their category perfectly. A character can
depart from the expected mold in some ways as long as other things are right.

**3. Get expert readers.** *Skyward* improved substantially because he brought in actual fighter
pilots and asked what he was getting wrong.

**4. Get three or four, not one.** People are individuals; living with a given condition or
identity differs person to person.

**5. Consider harm.** A popular author perpetuating a stereotype can genuinely damage readers
who already deal with misrepresentation.

**His outcome:** in every case where he made changes from this feedback, the story got better
**for all readers** — not just the group in question. That's the argument that matters.

## Checks

- [ ] Can you predict what this character does in an unplanned situation?
- [ ] Run the three-character no-tags exercise. Can you tell them apart?
- [ ] What deeper trait does each quirk express?
- [ ] Are you signaling intelligence with vocabulary instead of thought structure?
- [ ] For difficult characters — interesting, or just unpleasant?
- [ ] Want vs. need: what's the gap?

## Failure modes

- **Interchangeable voices.** The tag-free test catches this immediately.
- **Free-floating quirk.** Attached to nothing underneath.
- **Vocabulary-signaled intelligence.** The most common way smart characters read as fake.
- **Heavy dialect.** Exhausting to read.
- **Voice as excuse.** Structure abandoned for a voice that isn't strong enough to carry it.
