# Motivation, Personality, Values

**Source:** Sanderson, [Customizing Your Character, Lecture 6 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/customizing-your-character-brandon-sandersons-writing-lecture-6-2025) · **Tier:** PRIMARY
**Reach for this when:** building a character, or a character feels generic or inconsistent.

---

## The triad

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| **Motivation** | What does the character want? |
| **Personality** | *How* do they express what they want? |
| **Values** | What principles guide them? |

**Motivation is the foundation** — his words, first and most important. Everything else is
expression of it.

---

## Motivation

### Discovery questions

His question set for finding it:

- What in this character's history informs their current decisions?
- What key decisions have shaped them?
- **What don't they want people to know about them?**
- **What failure will they never repeat?**
- How do they handle blaming themselves for failures?

The middle two are the sharpest. A character with a concealed thing and a failure they won't
repeat has motivation, an internal conflict, and a flaw in one stroke.

### His process — worth knowing

He does **not** outline characters the way he outlines plot and worldbuilding. He writes them
and searches for the three elements in the writing, testing different expressions.

**Characters usually take several attempts:**
- Three versions of Vin before it worked
- Two Kaladins
- Dalinar worked immediately — too stubborn to be anyone else

**The permission in this:** a character not working in draft one is normal, not a failure. Even
for him, most take two or three tries.

## Personality

**Definition:** how they pursue their motivations. This is the layer readers connect with most
directly.

### Dimensions to examine
- Aggressive or passive in pursuit?
- Direct, or do they work desires into conversation sideways?
- Meticulous planner, or improviser?
- How do they treat others while pursuing what they want?

### The illustration that makes it click

**Same motivation, three personalities.** A character who loves their family expresses it as:

1. Quality time and shared meals — values emotional connection
2. Working extra hours to provide — values security
3. Fierce protectiveness, teaching self-defense — values safety and preparedness

Same want. Three entirely different people. **This is the single most useful character exercise
in the material:** take the motivation, generate three expressions, pick the one that isn't
obvious.

### The stereotype-to-nuance progression

Every character starts as a stereotype crossed with an archetype. Nuance is added as the story
goes.

**The permission:** stop trying to make a character three-dimensional at first appearance. It
doesn't work that way, even for him.

## Values

**Definition:** principles that shape both the motivation and its expression. This is what makes
villains comprehensible.

**Magneto.** Values: protecting his people, preventing genocide, trauma-driven fear. He believes
humans will do to mutants what was done to him, so he strikes first. Horrifying, and you
understand exactly what's driving it.

**Sauron — the counterexample.** Detailed motivation wouldn't strengthen the story. His job is to
be a vast force to be reckoned with, and that job doesn't require interiority.

**The lesson:** not every antagonist needs comprehensible values. Decide which kind you have, and
commit. A half-explained Sauron is worse than either option.

**Gollum — the balancing move.** Tolkien anchors the abstract evil with a highly relatable
character who just wants the ring, because he's been corrupted. Internally consistent without
being morally justified.

---

## The iceberg

> The reader only sees what's above water, but as writers we need to know the whole thing.

Personality is what readers fall in love with. It has to be anchored by everything underneath —
the motivation and values they never see stated.

**Practical consequence:** readers *feel* when a character isn't proactively moving the story,
but they'll attribute it to personality rather than mechanics. Character problems present as
character complaints, and the actual cause is usually structural.

---

## Checks

- [ ] What does this character want? One concrete sentence.
- [ ] What don't they want anyone to know?
- [ ] What failure will they never repeat?
- [ ] How do they *express* wanting it — and did you pick the non-obvious expression?
- [ ] What values shape both?
- [ ] For an antagonist: comprehensible values, or deliberate force-of-nature? Committed either
      way?
- [ ] If they're a force of nature, is there a Gollum somewhere to anchor it?

## Failure modes

- **Motivation-free character.** ~60% of character problems. See
  [proactive-relatable-capable.md](proactive-relatable-capable.md).
- **Obvious expression.** The first way you thought of to express the want. Generate three.
- **Stated but unenacted values.** They say what they believe; their choices don't reflect it.
- **Half-explained villain.** Neither comprehensible nor elemental.
- **Expecting draft one to work.** It usually takes two or three passes.
