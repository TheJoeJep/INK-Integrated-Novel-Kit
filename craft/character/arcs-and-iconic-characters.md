# Arcs and Iconic Characters

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 6.5 — *Types of Character Arcs* (web-exclusive Q&A), with
Lectures 5 and 6 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Lecture 6.5 has no official written notes** — this is the only record of it.
**Reach for this when:** designing an arc, deciding whether a character needs one, or planning
growth across a series.

---

## What an arc is

**Movement up or down one of the three axes** — see
[proactive-relatable-capable.md](proactive-relatable-capable.md).

- **Most common: up on relatability**, by visibly working on a flaw. The *working* raises
  relatability before the flaw resolves; resolution is the moment readers cheer
- **Second: up on capability** — Luke, from failing with a training remote to the trench run

**If you can't name which axis is moving, you don't have an arc** — you have a character who has
experiences.

**His recommended habit, which he rates above any taxonomy:** keep a running list of arcs you
notice in what you read and watch. What defines each, what makes it work, which ones you like.

*(And the companion habit — a private notebook of interesting flaws observed in real people, and
whether they overcame them. He calls it his black book.)*

## Arc types

### The apprentice arc

An apprentice follows a master. **The interesting friction is where they diverge**, not where
they follow:

- What can the apprentice do that the master can't?
- What can the master do that the apprentice can't?
- How must each approach the same problem differently?

**Especially good for differentiating two characters who share a skill set** — one learns from
the other and makes it their own.

### The classic flaw arc

Show early and concretely **how the flaw costs the character** — missed opportunities, stalled
growth, relationships breaking. You cannot pay off a cost you never established.

**Most flaws are double-edged.** His example: hubris is also self-confidence. That's what makes
them worth keeping until the end.

### The tragic version

Same setup — but the character reaches the exact moment where they **know** this is their tragic
flaw, and **refuses to let go of it anyway.**

**The distinction matters.** This is not "arrogance causes failure in act three." The character
must have already learned the lesson and consciously rejected it.

### The redemptive version

Identical setup. At the decision point, they choose differently.

### The mystery / ignorance-to-knowledge arc

The character learns about themselves while learning about the world. **What they uncover forces
them to become someone different in order to use it.**

**His example:** Agatha Christie's final Poirot novel — Poirot learns there are villains he can
prove nothing against though he's certain of their guilt, and the arc is what that does to him.

---

## The paint-by-numbers warning

He rejects the mechanical version outright. *Fail three times, then succeed* is **not** what makes
an arc work.

> What matters is the **why** — why has this character become someone who would choose
> differently now, and does the reader believe it?

## Iconic characters

**Characters with primarily external arcs**, or small internal ones that don't fundamentally
change them book to book. The kind of series you can read out of order.

**He states plainly these are just as valid** as arc-driven protagonists.

### Three reasons to use one

**1. Villains.** Iconic characters make excellent villains. You usually won't get their viewpoint
— his rough estimate is 60/40, hedged as approximate.

**The key move:** an iconic villain can have **already gone through the learn-it-then-refuse-it
beat before the story starts.** That refusal is part of what makes them a villain.

> **Joker vs. Gollum.** The Joker is fully iconic — decisions made, philosophy set. Batman's
> conflict *reveals* his flaws but doesn't change him, and that stasis is his downfall; he even
> lampshades it. Batman, meanwhile, does change. Gollum is less iconic — actively struggling with
> an arc — and makes the corrupted choice.

**2. Mentors.** Gandalf is iconic and doesn't change, while Frodo runs a **downward corruption
arc**, slowly destroyed by the Ring despite his best efforts. Gandalf already refused it; his
transformation with the Balrog happens *to* him rather than *because of* him.

**But mentor ≠ iconic.** He flags **Kelsier** explicitly as a counter-example so the two don't get
conflated.

**3. Sidekicks and ensemble.** The *Mission: Impossible* team mostly don't have arcs — their job
is continuity, laughs, and emotional beats, while the weight sits on Ethan Hunt plus usually one
villain and one secondary protagonist who *do* run arcs.

## Arcs across a series

### The backslide warning

**Don't have a character relitigate the same internal problem book after book.** You can maybe get
away with it once.

**The exception, done well:** Raimi's *Spider-Man* 1 and 2. Film one, Peter decides to be a hero —
expected. Film two is about **living with that choice**, and the cost being deeper than he
expected, so he questions it. He credits this as the strongest film of the trilogy *because* of
that escalation.

**His caution:** most writers who attempt a repeat backslide don't pull it off as well. He
doesn't recommend trying it twice.

### The Star Wars escalation model

Applied to character arc rather than plot:

| Film | Beat |
|---|---|
| **One** | Vader introduced as a villain — Luke never faces him directly |
| **Two** | Luke faces Vader and **loses** |
| **Three** | Luke faces Vader *and* the Emperor, escalated, and wins |

**The transferable question:** what small, real victory can you give a character in book one —
Luke wins by outflying, and by having better friends — **while leaving the larger challenge
visibly unresolved?**

### Other series tools

- **External arcs can substitute for internal ones** as a series goes on. Growth doesn't have to
  be interior forever
- **The Craig Bond model** — light-touch continuity. Bond is slightly different at the end of each
  film without every installment demanding a full explicit arc, and earlier films still matter
- **Vary what each book asks.** Avoid learning the same lesson twice — but also avoid making every
  book a maximally difficult internal arc

---

## Choosing

Ask first: **is this an iconic character or an arc character?** That determines whether "complete
arc in book one, more in sequels" is even the right frame.

**Don't give everyone an arc.** In a large cast it flattens the book — every thread runs the same
kind of change and none stand out. An iconic fixed point is what makes a moving one legible.

---

## Checks

- [ ] For each significant character: arc or iconic? Chosen deliberately?
- [ ] For arc characters — **which axis moves**?
- [ ] Has the flaw's **cost** been shown before you try to pay it off?
- [ ] Is the flaw double-edged, or purely a defect?
- [ ] For a tragic arc — does the character *know* and refuse anyway?
- [ ] Can you answer **why** they'd choose differently now, convincingly?
- [ ] Does everyone in the cast have an arc? (Usually a problem)
- [ ] For a villain — could they have already refused the lesson before page one?
- [ ] In a sequel — are they relearning something? Is this the one time you can afford it?
- [ ] What's the small real victory in book one, with the larger challenge left standing?

## Failure modes

- **Undeclared arc.** Change with no axis — reads as inconsistency
- **Paint-by-numbers.** Three failures then success, with no *why*
- **Uncosted flaw.** Never shown to hurt them, so resolving it means nothing
- **Arc inflation.** Everyone transforms; nothing stands out
- **The repeat backslide.** Relearning the same lesson in book three
- **Mentor/iconic conflation.** Assuming a mentor can't have an arc — Kelsier does
