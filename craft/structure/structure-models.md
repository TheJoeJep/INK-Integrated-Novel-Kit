# Structure Models

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 3 — *Story Structures* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** diagnosing a draft that's wrong in a way you can't name, or building an
outline.

---

## What a structure is for

> The conflict and the struggle is what makes a story — not the sequence of facts.

**The old adage he uses:** *the king died* is not a story, it's a fact. *The king died and then
the queen died of grief* is a story. Neither is really a story, but the point stands — events
aren't a story. What happens to characters emotionally as they work through struggle is.

**A survey, not a doctrine.** He presents these as tools to investigate and try, not a system.
His stated caveat before starting:

> The best method to write a story is the one that makes you consistently produce the type of
> thing you want to be producing, up to your current skill level.

---

## The obstacle rule — the most portable idea here

Every obstacle should do at least one of three things:

| | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Force** growth | In the character, or in the plot |
| **Reinforce** growth | Confirm a breakthrough was real, or that a clue actually matters |
| **Show failure** to grow | The character isn't ready yet — which highlights the problem |

> If an obstacle doesn't do those things, what you've added is just an event. Too many of those
> and it stops feeling like a story.

**His example:** the characters aren't ready to face Darth Vader. So Vader appears, Obi-Wan steps
in and is killed. The obstacle is survived — and growth is *forced*, because the person holding
them together is gone. Gandalf does the same job in *Lord of the Rings*.

**Use it as a filter.** For any scene that feels like filler, ask which of the three its obstacle
performs. If none, that's the diagnosis.

## Progress can be regressive

Backsliding counts. *Breaking Bad* is entirely backward progress — and it works, because the
author's goal is to show the slide. The story moves forward as the character moves down.

## Dan Harmon's Story Circle — his preferred model

He uses this instead of the monomyth, because it boils the same idea down to something simpler
and more flexible.

1. **A character at rest.** Either content (Bilbo — food, pipe, no problems) or wanting change
   but believing it impossible (Luke — I want the stars, my life will never change)
2. **Propelled by a need** they don't feel able to fulfil — **and they resist it.** Luke is
   offered the lightsaber and refuses
3. **Crossing the threshold.** A point of no return. Luke returns to find his aunt and uncle
   dead; that life has ended
4. **The road of trials.** All of the first half of act two. The character is tested and learns
   they lack the knowledge or skill
5. **Meeting with divinity.** A direct confrontation with the darkness inside — the Dagobah cave,
   where Luke cuts off Vader's head and finds his own face. Usually a parental figure
6. **The Elixir**, paid for with a price
7. **Master of two worlds**, spiritual and physical
8. **Return**, changed, bringing it back

**Why the circle shape matters:** stories do well when a character returns to where they were and
the reader can *see* the difference.

> Payoff can simply be: I was unable to do this at the start, and now I'm a master of it.

## The Hero's Journey — and his warning about it

He's read Campbell and finds value in it. He also passes on the criticism from his folklore
training: it doesn't reference its sources, uses others' work without attribution, works too hard
to prove its point, ignores counterexamples, and largely pretends women don't exist. Cinderella
fits it perfectly and is rarely discussed.

> Knowing that *Hero with a Thousand Faces* tries too hard is a really useful thing to know.

### The Lucas cautionary tale

**The centrepiece warning of the lecture**, and he was in the room for it.

Lucas loved Campbell and used him to great effect — and then, in Sanderson's reading, **turned him
into a checklist.** The monomyth calls for divine parentage. So *The Phantom Menace* gives Anakin
a virgin birth.

He was at the opening-night screening. The audience — people who had brought lightsabers — went
*oh*. Collectively. At that moment.

> Anytime a structure becomes too rigid for you as a writer, you can start using even your own
> outline to the point that it negatively impacts your story. If it doesn't match your story
> once you're in it, be willing to throw it away.

Same warning applies to three-act, and to *Save the Cat* — you'll read it and think you need a
specific beat on page sixteen. You're writing prose. Nobody checks.

### Chaining circles

Very feasible, and part of why *Star Wars* works. The first film is a complete circle **and**
act one of a trilogy — at the end Luke has only just accepted the calling, which is threshold-
crossing for the larger arc. Film two is failure. Film three completes the journey, with the
Elixir being that he redeems the father rather than becoming him.

### The tragic version

Greek tragedy is the character **refusing the last step** — refusing the Elixir, refusing to
return. Campbell covers this. He's on record predicting the *Kingkiller* trilogy will resolve this
way: a tragic flaw highlighted repeatedly through the road of trials, which the character refuses
to release, causing disaster instead of salvation.

## Three-act structure — which is really four

> It's three acts, but the first and last are short and the middle is double length with a big
> beat in the middle. That really feels like four acts.

Where the Story Circle is a circle, three-act is **an arrow pointed at an explosion.** Works
better for shorter novels than long ones.

### Act One

Exposition, character, need, status quo. Contains:

**The inciting incident** — the thing that starts a cascade. In *Star Wars*, Leia sending the
droids: droids land → Luke finds them → Luke ends up with Obi-Wan → so he isn't home when the
stormtroopers kill his family → so he must leave. He notes he isn't sure you *need* one.

**Plot point one** — life can never be the same. Separates act one from act two.

### The Major Dramatic Question

**He got this from Joe Russo**, one of the directors of the Avengers films. Asked what his single
most important storytelling tool was, Russo said: the dramatic question.

> What question will this story answer — particularly in relation to the character?

Russo told him every story he's made benefited strongly from knowing that question up front.

**It must be asked by the end of act one.** *Star Wars*: will Luke become a Jedi and avenge his
father? Asked at the moment he finds his aunt and uncle dead.

Note the cold open isn't act one — *Raiders* asks *will Indy find the Ark*, not *will he escape
this cave*.

### Act Two

> Act one you chase someone up a tree. Act two you throw rocks at them.

Rising action. Everything the characters try makes it worse. They lack the skill or knowledge to
solve it yet — which is where progress lives.

### The midpoint — the most important beat

In Hollywood structure, the single most important moment in the story. **It does not have to be a
surprise.** Three options:

- A **major escalation**
- A **reframing** of the story in some important way
- Making it **deeply personal** to the main character in a way it wasn't before

*Star Wars*: Alderaan is gone and they're pulled into the Death Star. The story stops being
*deliver these plans* and becomes *we are inside the problem*.

> It's the point where everyone who was watching and was just a little bit whatever sits up and
> says *oh*.

Then act two part two: attempts get more frantic, consequences of failure get greater.

### All Is Lost

The darkest moment, just before act three. Everything tried, no answers, we have failed — and
then something learned along the way reveals one last thing to try.

### Act Three

The last chance. No more trying. If you fail now, it's over. Then the climax the whole arrow has
pointed at — Luke exploiting the flaw in the Death Star while accepting his destiny by rejecting
technology and embracing tradition.

## Tropes — stop worrying about them

Asked when tropes become a problem:

> There is a certain madness to trying to avoid all tropes.

If you build an identity around not using them, you'll finish and discover you used different
ones, because certain things simply work.

**His own example, against himself:** he wrote *Mistborn*, delighted with a magic system where
people push off coins and fly. He brought it to his writing group. Their response: *that's
Magneto.*

> Four years of my life to write Magneto, but worse.

The stories are different. *The Lion King* is *Hamlet* and nobody minds.

> It's a bigger deal in first books than it ever is in second books. Later you start mastering
> your influences rather than unconsciously using them.

And a direct recommendation for a first novel: **you could do far worse than leaning hard on one
of these structures** — even too hard. Character and worldbuilding will stretch further
naturally, and that's where the book becomes yours.

## Levelling up — how to actually use all this

**His best framing for the whole craft base**, borrowed from Magic: The Gathering player Luis
Scott-Vargas.

When you learn a card game, the rules take all your attention — you have everything you can
manage just remembering when you draw. As those become second nature, brain space frees up, and
you can consciously focus on higher-level play. The better you get, the more becomes instinct.

Writing is the same.

> As a new writer, the hardest thing is doing it consistently — just sitting down and writing.
> That's often all you can do. If you try to keep all of this in mind, you'll be overwhelmed and
> won't get anything done.

**So:** build the habit first. Then add one thing to focus on. When that's instinct, add another.

Most of this is a **diagnostic kit for later**, or an outlining aid — not a checklist for the
next sentence.

## Checks

- [ ] Does every obstacle force, reinforce, or show failure in growth?
- [ ] Can you state the Major Dramatic Question as a question, in one line?
- [ ] Is it asked by the end of act one?
- [ ] Is there a real midpoint — escalation, reframing, or personal stakes?
- [ ] Is act two actually rising, or just continuing?
- [ ] Is there an All Is Lost, and does the way out come from something learned earlier?
- [ ] Are you using a framework as a diagnostic, or filling it in as a checklist?
- [ ] Is any structural element in there because the model demands it rather than the story?
