# Try-Fail Cycles, Yes-But / No-And, and Points on the Map

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 3 — *Story Structures* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
Try-fail attributed by him to **Dave Wolverton**, who taught him.
**Reach for this when:** the middle sags, problems resolve too easily, or you're discovery
writing and need an engine rather than an outline.

---

## Yes-But / No-And

> **⚠️ Attribution correction.** In the 2020 course Sanderson credits **Mary Robinette Kowal** for
> yes-but / no-and. The 2025 lectures present it without attribution, and this doc previously
> carried it as his. It isn't.
>
> She also teaches the **fuller four-way matrix** — adding *no-but* and *yes-and* — in her own
> guest lecture. See [../short-fiction/short-fiction.md](../short-fiction/short-fiction.md).

**The engine.** Stories are a series of **escalating** incidents, not just incidents — and
escalation produces a natural sense of progress, because it can only escalate so far before it
comes to a head.

### The full four-way version (Kowal)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Yes, but** | Progress — but pushed back, or it costs something |
| **No, and** | No progress, and worse |
| **No, but** | Failure with a consolation |
| **Yes, and** | Progress, and more besides |

**Yes-And is the closing-mode signal.** When beats stop trading progress for setbacks, you've
structurally entered the final movement — which makes the matrix a **position indicator**, not
just a scene-ending tool.

For each problem, ask: **does the character solve this?**

- **Yes, but** — they solve it, and a complication arrives
- **No, and** — they fail, and it gets worse

> The whole goal is to keep making things worse and worse and worse.

### The full *Star Wars* walkthrough

He runs it end to end, and it's the clearest demonstration of the technique:

| Problem | Answer |
|---|---|
| I need a droid | **Yes, but** it's the wrong droid |
| — and it has a secret message for Obi-Wan | Deliver it |
| Find Obi-Wan | **No, and** you're hunted by the Empire and your aunt and uncle are dead |
| Get off planet | **Yes, but** the pilot is a scoundrel who's only in it for money |
| Reach Alderaan and deliver the plans | **No, and** you're pulled into the Death Star, where there's a giant evil space wizard who killed your father |

**Start:** I need a droid. **End:** a military operation against the Galactic Empire, blowing up
the most destructive station in the galaxy, having become a space wizard.

> How did that happen? Small escalations.

### Screwball comedies use the same machine

They spiral until the absurd ending doesn't feel outlandish, because every step was small. A film
that begins with a man on a business trip with his fiancée ends with mobsters, millionaires, and
gunfire — and it tracks.

### The length limit

**Works best in shorter fiction.** Escalating across 400,000 words is much harder than across
80,000.

> It's a reason a lot of thrillers are in that 80–100k range. If they go longer, that constant
> escalation runs out of gas.

**But it scales down beautifully.** Use it tactically: *I need to get them from point A to point
B — let's do some yes-but/no-and.* Including on character problems: they need to learn to be more
caring. Do they manage it here? *No, and.* Here? *No, and.* A smaller version? *Yes, but.*

## Points on the map — Robert Jordan's method

**The hybrid that most discovery writers actually use.**

His metaphor: I know I'm driving to Washington DC. I know I want to see the Lincoln Memorial. I
also know I want to stop in Lincoln, Nebraska on the way.

So the outline is **six bullet points.** Character starts here, big event, big event, big event,
ends here. Then:

> I'm going to discovery-write between points one and two and see how long it takes me.

**Why it's worth doing even if you hate outlining:** knowing the big moments lets you start
**foreshadowing them.**

### The Jordan story, which is a warning as much as a method

Jordan pitched *The Wheel of Time* as **three books.** The ending in that three-book outline is
the ending Sanderson eventually wrote — in **book fourteen.**

He didn't add more on top. It just took fourteen books to get through three, because he kept
yes-but/no-and-ing all the way.

One of his mapped points: *a person pulls a sword that is not a sword from a stone that is not a
stone* — a King Arthur beat nobody clocks until much later.

**He suspects Rowling worked this way too**, despite her claims of more extensive planning —
because the things she foreshadows well are exactly the kind of thing points-on-the-map produces.

### The escape hatch

> If you're naturally a discovery writer — you can fix anything in post.

Revision can add all of this once you've reached the end. It's fine not to have it going in.

## The try-fail cycle proper

**Before something can be accomplished, a character should generally fail at it at least once.**
Depending on difficulty, twice or three times.

### The rule that makes it work

> They should try the most intelligent things you can think of, and execute them cleverly.

And then fail because of:
- **A personal flaw**
- **Lack of skill**
- **Being out-thought** — the villain already assumed someone would try the obvious thing

### The function most people miss

This is the part that isn't about the character at all:

> All the readers are thinking *well why don't you just do this*. The character tries it and
> fails — to prove to the reader that the author has thought of that, and that the problem is
> bigger than that.

**A try-fail cycle is reader management.** It's how you retire the obvious solution before the
reader starts distrusting the story for ignoring it.

Related: sometimes your first try-fail cycle exists purely to eliminate a plot-hole question.

### The Luke example, corrected

He first tries forcing *Star Wars* into try-fail at the plot level, decides it doesn't fit
(because Lucas was using a different structure), and relocates it — which is instructive:

**The cycle is Luke learning the Force, not the Death Star run.**

1. Offered the path of the Jedi — refuses, runs home to the power converters
2. Has the lightsaber, can't block the remote, isn't good enough
3. Vader arrives; Luke isn't ready; Obi-Wan intervenes and dies
4. Even at the end, Luke doesn't defeat Vader — he only takes the first step

> If Luke hadn't faced Vader, got his hand cut off, and failed, that whole trilogy would not be
> as successful or as interesting. That failure taught him so much.

**Obstacles force you to learn, if they're used correctly.** See the obstacle rule in
[structure-models.md](structure-models.md).

# Try-succeed cycles — when failure isn't the engine

**Source: 2025 Lecture 4.5 (Plot FAQ) · PRIMARY**

Asked directly whether there's a case for **try-succeed** instead of try-fail — since Paul
Atreides succeeds at nearly everything he attempts — he says yes, and gives three mechanisms that
substitute for failure.

## 1. The hidden tragedy — *Dune*

Paul fails once, enormously, at the start: he can't save his father and is exiled. After that he
succeeds at everything, all the way to the end.

**It works because the success is the problem.**

> You're slowly coming to realize that you are not watching a hero's origin story. You're
> watching a villain's origin story.

Paul resists becoming like the Harkonnens in order to bring them down — and fails at *that*. He
builds an army bent on vengeance and unleashes it on the galaxy.

**Structurally this is the gift metaphor at book scale.** You're promised the car — revenge — and
across the story you're shown you shouldn't have wanted it. See
[../plot/payoff-and-twists.md](../plot/payoff-and-twists.md).

> Maybe there was no better solution, but it's still terrible, and that's heartbreaking.

He calls it a master class, and warns it's **very dangerous to attempt** — but it makes a book
stand out when it lands.

## 2. Competence under sufficient danger — the Dirk Pitt model

Some books are built on a highly competent character facing external conflicts and largely
winning.

**Why it works:** make the problem big enough, the threats dangerous enough, and the stakes dire
enough that failure means death and destruction — and play the competence *very* competently.

> When you watch a trapeze act, you know they're not going to fall. They never fall. But you
> always wonder if they will. That's the question.

The reader isn't waiting for failure; they're waiting to find out whether *this* is the time.

**The related danger he flags:** readers bond to competence, which is why audiences sometimes
attach to villains — the villain is competent while the hero is still meandering toward
competence. See
[../character/proactive-relatable-capable.md](../character/proactive-relatable-capable.md).

## 3. Scope expansion

The character succeeds — genuinely, no failure — and then discovers the goal was smaller than the
problem.

**His example:** Frodo gets the ring to Rivendell. Near-failures, but no actual failure. And then
he learns it has to go very much further.

> This was only the prelude.

**Expansion of scope substitutes for failure** because the reader's sense of *how far there is to
go* resets upward.

## Choosing

| Use | When |
|---|---|
| **Try-fail** | The character must grow, and the reader needs the obvious solutions retired |
| **Hidden tragedy** | Success is the thing you want the reader to stop wanting |
| **Competence** | The pleasure on offer is watching someone excellent work |
| **Scope expansion** | Momentum matters more than growth, and the world is bigger than the goal |

## Checks

- [ ] Does the protagonist fail at least twice before anything important?
- [ ] Is each attempt the **most intelligent thing available** — would a smart person try it?
- [ ] Does at least one failure come from an internal flaw rather than bad luck?
- [ ] **Have you retired the obvious solution the reader is shouting?**
- [ ] Do the failures escalate?
- [ ] Does each scene end **yes-but** or **no-and**?
- [ ] If you're escalating across a long book — is the escalation actually sustainable, or will it
      run out of gas?
- [ ] Do you have points on the map, so you can foreshadow?

## Failure modes

- **Failing stupidly.** The reader sees the answer; now the character looks foolish
- **Failure by contrivance.** They fail because the plot needs it, not because the attempt was
  sound
- **The unretired obvious solution.** Readers spend the book asking why nobody tried the simple
  thing
- **Repetitive failure.** Three failures at the same difficulty
- **The clean solve.** First attempt works — no story in that beat
- **Escalation without a ceiling plan.** Everything maxed by the midpoint
