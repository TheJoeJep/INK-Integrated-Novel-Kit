# Escalation and Stakes

**Source:** Sanderson, [Overview of Story Structure, Lecture 3 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-overview-of-story-structure-lecture-3) · **Tier:** PRIMARY
**Reach for this when:** stakes feel unearned, a climax lands flat, or the story feels like it's
just getting louder.

---

## The core principle

> The conflict and the struggle make the story, not the sequence of events.

This is the sentence to keep. A plot outline is a sequence of events; a story is what those
events cost. Two manuscripts can have identical outlines and only one of them works.

## What escalation does

Escalating stakes force the character to grow, to fail, or both. Escalation without one of those
two outcomes is just volume.

## The timing problem

**His caution:** stakes that escalate too fast without buildup feel unearned.

> If your character goes from zero to saving the world in three chapters, the stakes won't feel
> earned.

**His negative example:** action films where the hero suddenly acquires competence after minimal
training. The payoff has nothing under it.

**His positive example:** *The Karate Kid*. Daniel's training is built step by step, so the final
victory is authentic. The film spends its runtime buying the ending.

**The general rule:** the length of the buildup determines the size of the payoff you can spend.
You cannot draw more than you deposited.

## Escalation is not only scale

The trap is treating escalation as *bigger* — more enemies, larger explosions, higher body count.
Escalation can also be:

- **Narrower.** The threat gets more personal and specific
- **More costly.** The same goal now requires giving something up
- **More compromised.** Success is still possible but no longer clean
- **More isolated.** The character loses allies, options, or information
- **Faster.** The same problem with less time

A story that only escalates in scale runs out of room. One that escalates in *kind* doesn't.

## Escalation and twists

From the 2020 material (SECONDARY): an escalation doesn't have to be a twist, but it usually
fills the same structural role.

**Practical consequence:** if a twist isn't working, try simply making things worse instead. It's
cheaper, more reliable, and doesn't require setup.

## Character arc via escalation

As external stakes rise, the character's understanding must deepen in parallel. His Star Wars
framing: Luke's challenges increase alongside his understanding of the Force, his role in the
Rebellion, and himself.

**The check:** for each escalation, name what the character now understands that they didn't
before. If nothing, the escalation is decoration.

---

## Checks

- [ ] Does each major beat cost more than the last?
- [ ] Is the escalation in *kind*, or only in scale?
- [ ] Was the buildup long enough to earn the current stakes?
- [ ] For each escalation — what did the character learn or lose?
- [ ] Could this beat be replaced with "the same thing, but worse," and would that be better than
      the twist you planned?
- [ ] Does the climax cost the protagonist something they'd have refused to pay in Act One?

## Failure modes

- **Volume escalation.** Louder, not higher.
- **Unearned stakes.** World-ending in chapter three.
- **Sudden competence.** Skills arriving when needed.
- **Static character under rising stakes.** Everything gets worse; nobody changes.
- **Escalation ceiling.** Everything is maximum by the midpoint and Act Three has nowhere to go.
