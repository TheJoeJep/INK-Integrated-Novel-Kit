# Genre Structure Mining

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 3 — *Story Structures* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** you have a story and no idea what shape it is, or a subplot has no
structure — and none of the standard models fit.

---

## The method

Instead of applying a general framework, **build your own from a specific sub-genre you love.**

He rates this highly for a specific reason:

> I really like this one simply because it allows you to practise your chef versus cook.

You're not inheriting someone's structure. You're deriving one, which means you understand why
every beat is there.

## The steps

1. **Pick a narrow sub-sub-genre.** Not *adventure*. Not even *spy movies*. **A specific kind of
   heist.**
2. **Collect everything you love that does it.** Books, films, across media.
3. **Study what makes the plot points work** — and which ones you don't like.
4. **Build the list of points** that recur and land. They don't all have to be present.
5. **Transplant it** into a different genre, medium, or aesthetic.

**Sample questions he models:** I love *Hamlet*. What's great about it? What other stories do
revenge, a person restoring themselves to their father's throne? Who else has done this? What can
I do with it?

---

## His worked example — and it's the one to remember

**These are all the same plot structure:**

- *Hoosiers*
- *Miracle*
- *Cool Runnings*
- *Pitch Perfect*
- *Ender's Game*
- **The bridge crew in *The Way of Kings***

The underdog sports story. A team who aren't good at what they're supposed to be doing, and a
coach who arrives, focuses them, and points them in the right direction so they can pull off the
impossible.

> The stakes are much bigger in *Way of Kings*. I don't think they're getting executed in *Pitch
> Perfect* very often.

**And he didn't do this quickly.** He spent *years* reading and watching these, working out which
points worked and why, until he could see the whole structure clearly enough to make it his own
and drop it into an epic fantasy.

---

## Why it works

**You can see the whole structure at once**, because you've seen it done so many times. That
solves the hardest problem in plotting — holding the shape in your head while writing.

**It produces originality for free.** Underdog sports plot + epic fantasy war camp is a
combination nobody reads as derivative, even though every beat is borrowed. This is the
[strange attractor](../process/idea-generation.md) applied at the structural level.

**It works for discovery writers too.** As he puts it: watch a bunch of these, build a plot out
of the points you like, ignore the ones you don't, adapt it into a new medium, and use it to
springboard the story.

## Where this fits with the other models

| Model | Comes from | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Story Circle | Campbell via Harmon | Character transformation |
| Three-act | Hollywood | Building toward a climax |
| Try-fail / points on the map | Wolverton, Jordan | Discovery writing forward |
| **Genre structure mining** | **You** | When nothing standard fits, or you want a shape nobody else has |

The others are borrowed. This one you build — which is why it's the one that trains judgment.

## Checks

- [ ] Is your chosen sub-genre **narrow enough**? "Heist" is probably still too broad
- [ ] Have you gathered enough examples to see what recurs, or are you working from one?
- [ ] For each recurring beat — do you know **why** it works, or just that it's there?
- [ ] Which beats are you deliberately dropping?
- [ ] Are you transplanting into a genre far enough away that it reads as new?
- [ ] Can you now see the whole structure at once?

## Failure modes

- **Too broad a source genre.** You get vague generalities instead of usable beats
- **Copying beats without understanding them.** This is cooking, not cheffing — and it's the exact
  failure the Lucas cautionary tale warns about
- **Transplanting too close to home.** Same genre, same aesthetic, and now it just reads as
  derivative
- **Skipping the study.** He spent years on his. A weekend of watching gets you a shallower
  version
