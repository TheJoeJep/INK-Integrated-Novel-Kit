# Scale, Length, and Scope

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 4.5 — *Plot FAQ* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** deciding how big a story is, or a book feels wrong in a way that's about
size rather than content.

---

## The governing rule

> **If you're going over around 120,000 words, the reader is going to expect an escalation in
> scope.**

It's hard to exceed 120k and still feel like a small-scale story. Length is itself a promise —
readers see the size and calibrate what they expect the book to be *about*.

**This cuts both ways.** A long book with small stakes disappoints. A world-ending premise in
70,000 words feels rushed.

## What counts as scope

Scope is not only *bigger threat*. Two ways to earn length:

- **Escalating stakes** — the *Harry Potter* path. It stops being *can I get good enough grades
  to stay a wizard* and becomes *can I save the world*, and the books lengthen in step
- **Breadth of time or life** — a family across generations, or one person's entire lifetime.
  Historical fiction earns length this way without any world-saving at all

## Rough figures he gives

| Work | Length | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Thrillers, most romance | 80–100k | Small scale sustained |
| *Dune* | ~170–180k | |
| *Lord of the Rings* | ~480k total, ~150k/book | "That's the epic scope" |
| *A Christmas Carol* | Novella | One person's life, hit very quickly |

---

## The reading-session insight

**This is the part that actually changes how you plot**, and it's the reason the word counts
matter rather than being trivia.

**80–100k is a one-sitting book.** Fast readers finish it in five to eight hours. That's why
thrillers live there:

> You can grab someone by the teeth and yank them through this story to the end so they're
> gasping.

Romance does the same thing with a different variety of gasp.

**An epic is read across days or months.** People *will* put it down. So you have to plot around
the fact that **break points exist**, and design where they fall rather than pretending they
don't.

### How he applies it

In *The Stormlight Archive* he writes **deliberately long chapters**, so that when one ends you've
had a complete piece of story and feel fine setting the book down.

**Except at climactic moments** — there he cuts to shorter chapters.

> Short chapters actually pull people through faster than long chapters do.

**So chapter length is a pacing control**, and it's one of the few that works at the structural
level rather than the sentence level:

| You want | Chapter length |
|---|---|
| The reader to be able to stop comfortably | Long — a complete unit of story |
| The reader unable to stop | Short — see [progress-and-signposting.md](../plot/progress-and-signposting.md) on thriller pacing |

## Knowing your own length

- **As a newer writer, you won't know.** That's normal — practice teaches it
- Professionals can usually tell **from the outline**
- Discovery writers often realize partway through: *this is going to take longer, I need to
  expand the scope*

**Note the direction of that fix.** When a book runs long, the answer is often to *expand the
scope* so the length is earned — not to cut back to the original size.

---

## Checks

- [ ] What's the target length, and does the scope match it?
- [ ] Over 120k — has the scope escalated, in stakes or in breadth of time?
- [ ] Under 100k — is this a one-sitting book, and is it paced to be read that way?
- [ ] Where are the natural break points, and did you choose them?
- [ ] Are chapters long enough to feel complete, except where you want no exit?
- [ ] Is chapter length varying with the intended pace, or uniform by habit?

## Failure modes

- **Long book, small stakes.** The commonest version of a book that "drags" without any specific
  bad scene in it
- **Epic scope in a one-sitting length.** Rushed; the payoffs aren't earned
- **Uniform chapter length.** Gives up a free pacing control
- **No designed break points** in a long book — the reader chooses them, badly
- **Cutting to fix length** when expanding the scope was the right answer
