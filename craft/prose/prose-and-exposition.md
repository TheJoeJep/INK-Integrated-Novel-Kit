# Prose and Exposition

**Source:** Sanderson, [Plot Q&A, Lecture 4 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/plot-q-a-brandon-sandersons-writing-lectures-2025) and [Worldbuilding Tools, Lecture 8 (2025)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025) (PRIMARY); 2020 course, Lecture 6 (SECONDARY)
Deep prose craft is a known [GAP](../SOURCES.md) — largely guest-lecture territory.
**Reach for this when:** handling exposition, or description feels flat or heavy.

---

## The spinach in the smoothie

His metaphor for exposition, and it's the right one:

> Exposition is the spinach in the smoothie.

The best worldbuilding doesn't overwhelm. It goes into dialogue, or through action, and readers
absorb it without noticing they were fed.

**The corollary people miss:** the spinach still has to be *in* there. The technique isn't
withholding information — it's suspending it in something else.

---

## The Pyramid of Abstraction

**The problem it solves:** every invented concept has to be communicated from scratch without
confusing the reader.

**The structure:**
- **Base** — concrete, specific, sensory description that grounds the reader
- **Apex** — abstract concepts, themes, generalizations

**The rule:** concrete language *earns* abstract discussion. Ground the reader first, then you
can go high-level. Start abstract and there's nothing holding the reader up.

**His examples of the concrete base done well:**
- The shimmer of spice in *Dune*
- The metallic taste of burning metals in *Mistborn*

Both give you a physical sensation before they give you a system. Describe how the magic
**feels**, not how it works.

## Info dumps

**Don't open with an encyclopedia entry.** The most common version: chapter one explaining the
world before anyone has a reason to care.

**The alternative:** convey setting through a character's eyes so the description does double
duty — it tells us about the world *and* exemplifies who's looking at it.

**The diagnostic question:** what does this character notice, and what does that reveal about
them? A soldier, a merchant, and a child walk into the same room and see three different rooms.

## Maid and butler dialogue

**Source: 2020, Lecture 6 · SECONDARY**

Characters explaining to each other things they both already know. Named for old stage plays
that used servants' conversation to deliver exposition.

It breaks immersion instantly, because nobody talks that way.

**The fix:** find a character who genuinely doesn't know. If nobody plausibly lacks the
information, the information probably doesn't belong in dialogue.

---

## Show vs. tell

**Source: 2020, Lecture 6 · SECONDARY**

Showing is more concrete than telling, but it costs more words.

**Not an absolute rule.** It's a trade between grounding and pace. Telling is the correct choice
for material the reader needs but shouldn't dwell on — and a manuscript that shows everything is
as badly calibrated as one that tells everything.

## Voice can outrank mechanics

From the character material: in some books, voice is doing the primary work and
promise-progress-payoff is well down the list — *Hitchhiker's Guide* being the extreme case,
where humor, message, and voice all outrank the mechanics.

**Treat this as a real option, not a general exemption.** It works when the voice is genuinely
exceptional. It is not a defense for a structureless manuscript.

See [voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md](../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md).

## Engaging writing comes first

From the 2020 material (SECONDARY), and worth stating plainly:

> People aren't going to get to your cool ideas if you can't write a great scene starting off.

The elaborate worldbuilding is downstream of a reader who is still reading.

## Checks

- [ ] Is any exposition sitting undissolved — a paragraph that stops the scene to explain?
- [ ] Is there concrete grounding before each abstract passage?
- [ ] Does description come through a specific perception, or a neutral narrator?
- [ ] What does what-this-character-notices reveal about them?
- [ ] Any maid-and-butler dialogue — two people saying what they both know?
- [ ] Have you described how it *feels* before how it *works*?

## Failure modes

- **The opening encyclopedia entry.** Chapter one explains the world.
- **Abstract without base.** High-level discussion the reader has no footing for.
- **Maid and butler.** Characters informing each other of shared knowledge.
- **Neutral description.** Setting rendered by nobody in particular — a wasted characterization
  opportunity.
- **Mechanics before sensation.** Explaining the system before the reader has felt it.
