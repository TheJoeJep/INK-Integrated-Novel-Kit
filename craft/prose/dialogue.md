# Dialogue — the MICRO framework

**Source:** Sanderson, 2016 Lecture 11 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**The only substantial dialogue teaching in the corpus.** No 2020 or 2025 lecture covers it at
this depth.
**Reach for this when:** dialogue reads stilted, characters sound alike, or a conversation scene
is inert.

> He presents this as "a work in progress" and calls it *this little acronym of mine.* The caption
> track never records him saying "MICRO" aloud — but the five terms in the order he gives them
> spell it, and the acronym is almost certainly deliberate.

---

## The five, in order of importance

**He ranks them explicitly, and the ranking is the most useful part:**

| | | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **M** | **Motivation** | **Most** — the top two |
| **I** | **Individuality** | **Most** — roughly co-equal |
| **C** | Conflict | |
| **R** | Realism | |
| **O** | Objective | **Least** — and the one new writers over-prioritize |

That last line is the whole diagnosis. **New writers optimize for the least important element.**

---

## M — Motivation

**The single most common cause of stilted dialogue in student work**, from his direct experience:

> Throwing character motive out of the window and letting plot necessity drive the conversation.

The character stops speaking as themselves and starts speaking as a delivery mechanism for
information the reader needs.

**The fix:** focus on what the character *wants in life* and why they're speaking the way they
are — not on what the outline needs conveyed.

---

## I — Individuality

**Definition:** you can tell characters apart with the tags stripped out, purely from how they
talk.

### Dialect and accent

The fullest treatment in the corpus. He polls the class on who enjoys thick written dialect —
essentially nobody raises a hand.

**So the guidance is: sparing, suggestive, not phonetic.** A few markers of rhythm and word choice
do more than respelling.

### The intelligence trap

The instinctive answer is *bigger words for smart characters*, and he flags it as overused.

**The reality check:** highly educated people don't use long words much in normal conversation.
What differs is how they **organize thought** — sentence complexity, ideas building on each other.
(Consistent with the Ken Jennings example in
[../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md](../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md).)

---

## C — Conflict

**Definition:** good dialogue carries innate tension. **This does not require characters to be
arguing.**

> Tension makes for good everything. Conflict makes for good everything. That's the glue.

**The mechanism:** characters should enter a conversation **wanting something** — information,
reassurance, a concession. That want generates tension even between allies.

### Fixing the worst case — the explaining scene

Two people, one knows, one doesn't, nothing at stake. Options raised:

- **Make the explainer reluctant** to give it up
- **Make either party unsure they can trust the other** — the explainer might suspect Watson of
  being a spy
- **Make it matter to the character, not just the reader** — which routes straight back to
  Motivation

### The worked example — BBC's *Sherlock*

It works because **both characters need something from each other**: Sherlock needs Watson's
humanity, Watson needs Sherlock's genius — and Sherlock doesn't consciously register his half.

The exchange he cites: Sherlock rattles off deductions, Watson makes him back up — *explain number
four again.* **The asymmetry of knowledge is itself the tension engine.**

---

## R — Realism

**Framed with an explicit asterisk: there is no universally correct realism level. Pick your target
and commit.**

Real speech is full of repetition, filler and false starts. Transcribed verbatim it's unreadable.

**His stylization example:** Shakespeare's contemporaries complained they couldn't follow the
plays, and the dialogue is considered masterful anyway.

> Joss Whedon is Shakespearean in his approach to dialogue.

Highly stylized, highly entertaining, not remotely naturalistic — and a legitimate target.

**Realism is the *second* most common cause of stilted dialogue.** But note: *"it doesn't feel
real"* is a symptom with several possible causes — missing motivation, missing individuality, or a
scene that reads as a polished set-piece speech rather than people talking.

**So don't take that note at face value.** Diagnose which of the three it actually is.

---

## O — Objective

What the conversation accomplishes for the plot.

**Least important of the five** — and the thing newer writers reach for first. Get the other four
right and the objective takes care of itself; get only the objective right and you have characters
reciting the outline.

---

## The exercise

From [../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md](../character/voice-quirks-and-dialogue.md), and
it's the direct test of Individuality:

**Write a three-character scene with no dialogue tags and no setting description.** If a reader
can't track who's speaking, Individuality has failed.

---

## Checks

- [ ] What does each character **want** in this conversation?
- [ ] Strip the tags — can you tell them apart?
- [ ] Is there tension, even though nobody's arguing?
- [ ] Is anyone explaining something with nothing at stake? Add reluctance, distrust, or personal
      cost
- [ ] What realism level are you targeting, and is it consistent?
- [ ] Are you signalling intelligence with **vocabulary** rather than thought structure?
- [ ] Is dialect suggested or phonetically spelled out?
- [ ] Are you writing to the **objective** — what the scene must convey — instead of to motivation?

## Failure modes

- **Plot-driven dialogue.** The commonest cause of stilted speech
- **Interchangeable voices.** The no-tags test catches it immediately
- **The inert explanation.** Nobody wants anything
- **Vocabulary-signalled intelligence**
- **Heavy phonetic dialect.** Almost nobody enjoys reading it
- **Optimizing the least important element.** Objective first, motivation last — backwards
