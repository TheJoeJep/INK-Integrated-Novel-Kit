# What Prose Does That Film Can't

**Source:** Sanderson, 2025 Lecture 4.5 — *Plot FAQ* · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**Reach for this when:** you've absorbed craft advice built on film examples and need to know
what translates — or you're not using the medium's actual advantages.

---

## Why so many examples are films

He uses them because everyone's seen them, they take two hours instead of fifty, and — the
useful reason — **films must condense, so they hit the beats more clearly.** A film is a
structural diagram of a story.

That's exactly why the advice needs translating rather than copying.

---

## Three advantages prose has

### 1. Inside the head

First person, third limited, even omniscient — all give you direct access to thought.

> In film, Luke has to look wistfully at the suns. You can't hear him think it.

In *Dune* you're inside Paul thinking about the dangers of Arrakis and missing his home planet.
**That creates connection immediately**, and no film technique substitutes for it.

**The failure mode, which he names:** *navel-gazing* — pages of a character ruminating. Everyone
writing prose is guilty of it, precisely because it's the medium's strength.

### 2. Space

A film has ninety minutes to three hours. It must hit beats hard and obviously.

A book can:
- **Slow burn**
- Have **more failures**, and more steps backward
- Run several scenes reinforcing the same idea
- Spend time on **side characters**
- Meander and still arrive

**For plotting specifically:** more failures and successes, more red herrings, and **twists can be
more spectacular** because there's so much more running at once to misdirect with.

### 3. No visuals — which makes the reader a participant

> In film they're an observer. In a book they're a participant.

You *have* to rely on the reader filling in blanks, and that's leverage. **Broad brush strokes** —
give a character one identifying feature and let the reader build the rest.

This is the advantage most easily wasted, by writers who try to specify everything the way a
camera would.

---

## What this means for craft advice

| Film-derived advice | Translating it |
|---|---|
| Hit the beat hard and clearly | You can afford subtlety and repetition — but don't lose the beat |
| Show, don't tell | You have interiority. Telling a thought is showing, in prose |
| Enter late, leave early | Still true, but you have room for more scenes overall |
| One clear want per scene | Same — but you can run the internal and external want simultaneously. See below |

---

## Layering internal and external conflict

**Something prose does especially well**, and one of his favourite techniques.

Give a viewpoint character **both** internal and external conflict in the same scene, and let them
interact. The requirements:

- **Signpost the internal conflict early** — make the promise, then show progress on it
- **Establish why the flaw is legitimately a flaw** — something they need to overcome or work
  around
- **Don't batch it.** Don't do all the character work in one block and then move on. Seed it
  through
- **The internal conflict should be causing the external trouble** through the middle of the
  story

### The danger, and the fix

> You don't want to make us dislike the character. That's the big challenge.

Readers get frustrated with a character whose flaw feels like something they should just deal
with. Three defences:

1. Make sure we **understand and like** them independently
2. **Relate the flaw to something human** we all recognize
3. **Have them — or the narrative — acknowledge it.** *I can't keep letting this stand in my way.*
   This is hanging a lantern on the flaw: yes, this is real, yes, you should be a little
   frustrated, be patient

**Or don't resolve it.** They can simply never get over it — readers will be frustrated, so lean
harder on other reasons to like them. A little frustration can be productive.

### The one-two punch

**The structure that makes it pay:** arrange for the problem the flaw caused to **happen again,
bigger**, where they can't afford to fail — and this time they overcome it.

**Luke:** stung on the ankle by the training remote because he won't trust the Force. Later, the
entire Rebellion dies if he doesn't. Same failure, raised stakes, and the payoff is him choosing
to trust.

He also notes the promise work in that same final scene: the film establishes that making the
shot without the Force is extremely difficult, and shows an attempt failing, *before* Luke's turn.

## Checks

- [ ] Are you using interiority, or writing a screenplay in prose?
- [ ] Any navel-gazing — rumination that isn't doing work?
- [ ] Are you over-specifying what the reader would rather fill in?
- [ ] Is character work **seeded through**, or batched into blocks?
- [ ] Is the internal conflict causing external trouble, or running parallel to it?
- [ ] Does the flaw get acknowledged, so frustration doesn't curdle into dislike?
- [ ] Is there a one-two punch — the same failure, later, at stakes they can't afford?
