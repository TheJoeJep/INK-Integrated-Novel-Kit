# Viewpoint

**Source:** Sanderson, 2020 Lecture 4 · **Tier:** PRIMARY (full transcript)
**No 2025 lecture covers viewpoint directly** — this is the only substantial treatment in the
corpus, apart from a viewpoint-and-tense section inside 2025 Lecture 8.
**Reach for this when:** choosing a viewpoint, or a manuscript feels distant, confusing, or
inconsistent in perspective.

> **⚠️ His sub-model names are his own invented vocabulary for the class**, not industry terms —
> he flags this himself, twice, and says *"I call it something different every year."* Same
> terminology churn as the flaw/handicap taxonomy. The **distinctions** are what matter; the
> labels are his teaching shorthand.

---

## The three standard viewpoints

**Omniscient, first person, second person.** In practice almost everyone uses the first two.

**Second person — actively discouraged** unless it will be a major selling point and you know
exactly what you're doing. It tends to land either "very literary and very difficult" or "very
pulpy."

---

## First person, three modes

### 1. Epistolary

Told through letters, journals, documents.

| Buys | Costs |
|---|---|
| **Built-in mystery.** Unlike flashback, it doesn't tip that the narrator survived — you're living it moment by moment | **Rigid form.** Structurally constraining, and some readers won't take to it |
| **Hugely immersive**, supports rich embedded lore and ephemera | **The verbatim-memory problem.** Characters transcribing exact dialogue into a letter strains credulity |
| **Easiest form for hiding information without it reading as a cheat** — *it just wasn't in that letter* | |

**His example of the form working:** *Watchmen*'s back-matter — he singles out a character's own
in-universe memo about his action-figure licensing as something that reveals enormous amounts
through document voice alone.

**His own use:** chapter epigraphs are often epistolary.

### 2. Flashback (classic first person)

The character narrates a past story.

**The defining advantage, and it's specific:**

> **This is the only mode where "pay attention, this matters later" doesn't feel like cheating.**

The narrator knows how it ended and is allowed to say so. In present-narrator mode the same move
reads as authorial intrusion.

**The defining cost:** the narrator obviously survived. Tension from *will they live* is gone.

**And a second, subtler one:** he notes it can tip tone — if the narrator is jovial about it, you
may have promised a comedy.

**Structural note:** *The Name of the Wind* inverts the usual pairing. Most mode-mixing books use
present-narrator or omniscient as the frame; there, the **frame is third limited**, and the
first-person flashback only begins when Kvothe sits down to tell it. He calls it an ingenious
frame story.

**Distinguished from epistolary:** it reads like a novel, with far more detail than anyone would
plausibly write in a journal. That's part of the affectation — the *Name of the Wind* audiobook
runs about forty hours for a story framed as told in one or two sittings.

### 3. Cinematic / immediate

Real-time narration, as though the character narrates their life as it happens.
**The current standard for YA.** His own *Skyward* and *Steelheart*.

More intimate than third limited, which by comparison feels a little more distant.

**Shares with flashback:** an unreliable narrator is far easier here. The narrator can say *it's
too painful, I'll get to it* and readers accept it, where the same withholding in third limited
feels like a cheat.

### The shared first-person payoff

> It covers a whole host of sins.

If the voice is genuinely interesting, a great deal is forgiven. And it reframes the story: *we
need to go to the place and get the thing* becomes *how is my character changing as they go* —
the thing stops mattering as much as what pursuing it does to them.

**He notes you can and should aim for that in third limited too** — first person just makes it
sing.

### The stacking problem

Two or three first-person narrators makes tracking harder, because you lose the repeated-name
orientation cue that comes free in third person — and the intimacy that makes first person work
starts to interfere once you're building that.

## Third limited

One character's perceptions per scene; switch between scenes.

**Buys:** credibility, manageable large casts, and the gap between **perception and reality** —
the reader can see the character is wrong. His example: Mat Cauthon in *The Wheel of Time*.

**Costs:** less intimate than first person, and **hiding information reads as cheating.**

### His admission against himself

Concealing Kelsier's secret in *Mistborn* through third-limited narration is, in his words,
**absolutely cheating** — and sometimes a necessary cheat.

Worth keeping, because it's the honest version: the rule is real, he broke it deliberately, and he
doesn't pretend otherwise.

---

## Omniscient

### Present narrator

A hybrid — a storyteller relating events, not confined to any head, moving around and reporting.

**The cost that matters:** *pay attention, this matters later* **does** read as cheating here, in a
way it doesn't in flashback.

### True omniscient

Access to all characters simultaneously. Distinctive, harder to manage, reads as less modern.

---

## Mixing modes deliberately

**The technique worth stealing.** In *Skyward* he seeds **third-limited interludes at chapter ends
throughout the book**, so that a late viewpoint switch at the climax doesn't feel jarring. *The
Martian* does the same thing.

**The principle:** if you'll need a mode shift late, establish the pattern early — one instance is
enough to make it a convention rather than a violation. Same logic as time-skips in
[../plot/progress-and-signposting.md](../plot/progress-and-signposting.md).

## Tense correlation

| Mode | Tends toward |
|---|---|
| Cinematic | Present tense |
| Epistolary, flashback | Past tense |
| Third limited (epic fantasy) | Past |
| First person intimate (YA) | Often present |

Conventions, not rules — but they are reader expectations, which makes them
[structural promises](../plot/promises.md).

## Multiple viewpoints

**Readers pick favourites, and he admits this is an unsolved problem** — "favourites
polarization." Some readers will skim a viewpoint. There's no clean fix.

**Two different situations:**

- **Characters together** — easier; shared scenes carry investment across
- **Characters separated from the start** — each thread needs its own promise and visible
  progress, or it reads as a diversion

**The failure case:** Finn and Rose in *The Last Jedi* — a separated thread the audience had no
investment in. **And he pairs it with his own parallel in *Oathbringer***, which he had to fix the
same way, by giving the viewpoint character a personal stake. See
[../plot/progress-and-signposting.md](../plot/progress-and-signposting.md).

**Scope rule:** viewpoints expand a novel by **distance, not count**. Two people on two continents
expand scope; two people in the same room don't. See
[../character/arcs-and-iconic-characters.md](../character/arcs-and-iconic-characters.md).

## Checks

- [ ] Which mode, specifically — not just "first person"
- [ ] What does it cost, and have you compensated?
- [ ] If flashback: tension can't come from survival. Where does it come from?
- [ ] If third limited: are you withholding something that will read as a cheat?
- [ ] Are you signalling *this matters later*? Only flashback gets that free
- [ ] Will you need a late mode switch? If so, is the pattern seeded early?
- [ ] Does each viewpoint have its own promise and visible progress?
- [ ] Are characters separated? Then each thread needs independent investment
- [ ] Do the viewpoints expand scope by distance, or just add count?
- [ ] Stacking two or three first-person narrators — can readers still tell them apart?

## Failure modes

- **Undeclared mode.** Drifting between immediate and retrospective first person
- **The cheat that shows.** Withholding in third limited or present narrator
- **The dead viewpoint.** A POV with no promise of its own — readers skim it
- **The unearned late switch.** A mode change at the climax with no precedent
- **Narrator stacking.** Three first-person voices and no way to track them
