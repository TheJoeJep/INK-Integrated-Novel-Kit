## Two audiences, two openings

The same book, pitched two different ways, because what makes someone lean in depends on what
they already know how to want.

| Audience | Leads with | Sanderson's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Genre-familiar** | The premise inversion — what's been flipped from something they already know the shape of | *What if Frodo reached the end, Sauron killed him, and took over the world? That's this world. Years later, a gang of thieves plots to rob the Dark Lord.* |
| **Genre-unfamiliar** | A specific character and what they discover or become | *This is about a young girl named Vin who discovers her lifelong streak of luck is actually a connection to a magic that comes from metal.* |

**Ask which situation applies** before drafting — a genre editor at a conference and a
non-reading relative at dinner need different openings on the same book. If unclear, draft both;
that's the point of the skill.

---

## Building the genre-familiar version

**Ask first: is there a genre convention or a well-known work this book inverts, subverts, or
answers?** Not every book has one — don't force an inversion that isn't there. If the honest
answer is "no clean inversion," say so and lead with premise straight, not manufactured contrast.

If there is one: state the familiar shape in one clause, then the flip, then the present-day
consequence — that's the three-beat structure the Frodo/Sauron example uses. Offer at least two
phrasings; the choice of what to compare against changes who nods along.

## Building the genre-unfamiliar version

**Ask: who is the one character a stranger to the genre would latch onto, and what do they
discover or become?** Not the protagonist's role in the plot — the human hook. Offer two or three
candidate openings if more than one character could carry it (a genre-unfamiliar listener may
respond better to the wounded dragon's situation than to the protagonist's, depending on the book).

---

## Comp titles

**Choose deliberately, not by proximity of genre.** For each candidate comp, ask what it signals:
tone, audience age, marketability, or a specific craft comparison ("the voice of X, the structure
of Y"). A comp chosen just because it's in the same genre says nothing.

**Only propose comps the author has actually read**, or ask which books they'd want to be
compared to — don't assert a comparison to a book the system hasn't been told the author knows.

## Credibility line

**Brief. One sentence, maybe two.** It should not overshadow the manuscript — this is a footnote
establishing the author isn't a stranger to the form, not a resume recitation. If there's nothing
substantial to say yet, it's fine to have no credibility line at all; a debut author's book is the
credibility.

---

## The conference technique

When the author is on the listening end instead of the pitching end — talking to an editor or
agent at a conference — **don't ask "what are you looking for?"** Everyone asks that, and it
produces a generic, hedged answer.

**Ask instead: "what have you bought recently by a new author, and why?"** It's specific and
answerable, and it reveals what someone actually acquires rather than what they say in the
abstract they want.

## Rules

- **Offer options, never a single pitch.** At least two phrasings per version.
- **Don't force an inversion that isn't there.** Say so rather than manufacturing false contrast.
- **Don't propose a comp the author hasn't confirmed reading.** Ask; don't assert.
- **Keep the credibility line brief and secondary.** The manuscript is the pitch, not the author's
  history.
- **Never invent a proper noun, character, or plot detail not already in `canon/` or `story/`.**
  If the pitch needs something that isn't settled yet, ask rather than filling it in.
- **This produces spoken/verbal material, not a document.** Don't write it into any file in
  `canon/`, `story/`, or elsewhere unless the author asks to save it.
