# /real-world — contemporary and historical settings

> section of this process — the masquerade problem — has no craft-base coverage, and this skill

**Say this on invocation:** *Working the real-world settings process in stages — the framework is

covered anywhere in the source material, and I won't improvise a position on it.*

**Source:** [`craft/worldbuilding/real-world-settings.md`](../../craft/worldbuilding/real-world-settings.md)
— the only lecture in the course addressing real-world settings; everything else in the craft base
assumes an invented world.

**Applies to:** contemporary fiction, urban fantasy, secret history, historical fiction — anything
where the reader can check you against a world they already know.

---

## Stage 1 — the core asymmetry

> Getting real-world detail wrong is far easier than getting invented-world detail wrong, and the
> odds an expert catches you go up enormously.

Two named examples: Jim Butcher gets complaints from Chicago residents about the *Dresden Files*
city layout, because he wasn't living there when he wrote it. WWII novels are notoriously hard to
get right because the population of armchair experts is vast.

**Why one error costs disproportionately:** once a reader catches a single fundamental mistake,
**they stop trusting your judgment on everything else** — including the character work. It isn't
proportional damage. It's a trust collapse, all at once.

**Ask:** what real-world domains does this story touch that a reader could check you on? List
them before doing anything else — the rest of this process runs per domain, not once for the whole
book.

---

## Stage 2 — bonehead characters

**The best technique in this material, and it's close to free.**

Give the viewpoint character a plausible, characterized reason not to know the domain, instead of
faking expertise you don't have.

**His example: Kaladin doesn't know horses.** Neither does Sanderson — his wife does. Rather than
fake it, he built a character trait around the gap.

**Why it works:** readers forgive a gap that's *characterized* — established as belonging to this
person, for a reason — but not one that's *accidental*, which reads as the author not knowing.
And a characterized gap stays correctable: the character can be taught in-story later, which is
itself a scene.

**Ask, per domain from Stage 1:** does research close this gap, or does a bonehead-character trait
close it instead? Either is legitimate. Faking it is the only wrong answer.

---

## Stage 3 — how much research is enough

**The model:**

| Effort | Gets you |
|---|---|
| First weeks/months | **~50% of the way to expertise.** Early gains are cheap |
| **~10× that again** | 50% → 75% |
| Beyond that | Micro-increments |

**The actual process, in order:**

1. Research until you're confident your likely mistakes are **fixable without undermining the
   narrative**
2. **Write the story**
3. Find a genuine expert to flag what's wrong, and revise on demand

Not years to expertise before drafting. Sanderson names two cases where this approach still bit
him — he underestimated his own competence and needed more correction than expected — so the
model isn't a guarantee, but it's still the cheaper failure mode than over-researching.

**Ask:** for each domain from Stage 1 — are you at roughly 50% and ready to draft, or already past
it and stalling? Is there a genuine expert reader lined up for the revision pass?

## Stage 4 — conscious vs. accidental

**When the story borrows from a real culture or belief system**, the operative distinction is
**not right vs. wrong** — it's whether the choice was made **consciously**, and can be defended, or
absorbed without noticing.

**His example against himself, worth using exactly because it cuts both ways:**

- **Bridge Four has no women** — a conscious, defensible choice: wartime forced-labour
  conscription. Another author could argue it differently. It was a *decision*
- **The Mistborn crew was entirely male** — he built Vin as a dynamic character, partly drawing on
  his 14-year-old sister, and only noticed *after finishing* that everyone else was a man. Absorbed
  from every heist film he'd seen. **Unconscious**, and already in print

Same surface fact. Completely different footing.

**His cardinal rule on borrowing specifically:**

> I want people to depict me well and do the research for me — so I want to make sure I'm
> depicting them well.

**Ask:** is anything here borrowed from a real, living culture or belief system? If so — is the
choice conscious and defensible, or is it something absorbed from other fiction that hasn't been
examined yet?

---

## Stage 5 — contrast

**For building:** two real-world-adjacent cultures or institutions land faster as **contrasting
facets of the same underlying thing**, or as opposed views on something central to the plot.

**For pitching:** the whole premise compresses to one line via contrast. His example — the film
*Yesterday*: the Beatles never existed, one man remembers them, nobody else does. That's the
pitch, in one sentence.

**Ask:** what's different between this world and ours, what's the same, and can the premise be
stated as one contrast line? And: is the learning curve here steep or shallow — that's a choice,
not a default, and it signals how demanding the book will feel from page one.

## The known gap — read this before offering anything on it

**The masquerade** — why the wider world hasn't noticed the fantastic element — and the mechanics
of alternate or secret history are **not covered anywhere in any of the 39 lectures.** This was
checked directly against three sources where it would naturally appear (this lecture, the 2025
Worldbuilding Q&A, the 2020 Worldbuilding Q&A) and all three came up empty.

The closest adjacent material — Saberhagen's *Books of Swords* in the 2020 Q&A, advanced humans
mistaken for gods — solves a **secret history inside an invented world**, not the real-world *why
hasn't anyone noticed* problem. It isn't the same question.

**If the story needs the masquerade solved: say plainly that this is unanswered in the source
material, and that any proposal is unsourced raw material, not a documented technique.** Don't
present it with the same confidence as the rest of this process.

## Rules

- **Run the stages in order**, once per domain touched where the stages call for it
- **Bonehead character or research — never neither, and never faked expertise.** Faked expertise
  is worse than an admitted gap, because it isn't characterized
- **Name conscious vs. accidental explicitly** for anything borrowed from a real culture or belief
- **Never improvise Sanderson's position on the masquerade.** Flag the gap, offer unsourced raw
  material if asked, and say clearly that it isn't sourced
- **Offer options, never one design.** Tier 4 content inside a Tier 3 process
- **Don't write the setting into docs unasked** — that's `/write` in Structure Mode
